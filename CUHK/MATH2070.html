MATH2070 Algebraic Structure

Introduction

Why do we study algebraic structure? Because we want to abstract and generalise theorems whenever possible.

In the following, we pay our focus on groups and rings, provide abstractions of many well-known stuff, for example set of integers, matrices, permutations or functions.

Group

A group is a set $G$ equipped with a group operation $\ast : G \times G \rightarrow G$, such that it is associative while both identity and inverse exists. Basically we want to have a set of conditions that is minimal so that it can apply to more different objects, but also the conditions should be "strong enough" for us to deduce something useful. We call a group abelian if the group operation is commutative, and the order $|G|$ of a group being the cardinality of $G$.

There are tons of examples, such as $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, $(\mathbb{Q}^\times, \cdot)$, $(U_m, \cdot)$ (Roots of Unity), $(S_x, \circ)$ (Bijective functions from $X$ to itself), or things like $\text{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (General Linear Group, set of $n \times n$ matrices with determinant non-zero), $\text{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (Special Linear Group, set of $n \times n$ matrices with determinant $1$) or $(\mathbb{Z}_n, +_n)$ (Modular Arithmetic). All these satisfy the condition being a group, and hence all the laws and rules deduced henceforth can be applied on them.

To abstract the concept of functions, we can consider categories as well. A category $\mathcal{C}$ consists of objects $\text{Obj}(\mathcal{C})$ and morphisms between objects $\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ where $A, B \in \text{Obj}(\mathcal{C})$ where identity exists, which is $1_A \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, A)$, and we can compose morphisms together, $(f, g) \mapsto g \circ f$ with $\circ$ associative. Examples includes the categories of set and vector spaces. Specially, we call a morphism $f \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ isomorphism if there exists $g \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(B, A)$ such that $g \circ f = 1_A$ and $f \circ g = 1_B$, where automorphism is isomorphism from $A$ to itself. Interestingly (actually from definition), the set of all automorphisms $\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(A)$ also forms a group!

Common Groups

Cyclic Groups are groups that can be generated by a single element, or $G = \langle g \rangle = \{ g^n : n \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ for some $g \in G$, which $g$ is called a generator of $G$ (which need not be unique). We define similarly the order of $g$, $|g|$, being the cardinality of $\langle g \rangle$. Somewhat obviously, we have $|g| = \infty \implies \langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $|g| \lt \infty \implies \langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_{|g|}$, where $G \cong H$ means $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic. Clearly all cylic group are abelian as well.

Symmetric Groups are groups containing permutations, which the elements of $S_X$ are $\sigma: X \rightarrow X$ where $\sigma$ are bijective. Definition tells us that $|S_n| = n!$, while $S_n$ is not abelian for all $n \ge 3$. We can denote cycles as $\begin{pmatrix} i_1 & i_2 & \cdots & i_k \end{pmatrix}$, which is the permutation mapping $i_j$ to $i_{j + 1}$ for all $1 \le j \le k - 1$ and $i_k$ to $i_1$, fixing all other values. Clearly we can decompose every permutation into disjoint cycles, which then we can consider the number of inversions. Denote $A_n$ the alternating group which contains all permutations that (the number of inversions) is even, we know that $A_n$ is simple for all $n \ge 5$, that is, it does not contain proper normal subgroup.

Dihedral Groups are groups mimicking rotating and reflecting a regular polygon, where the elements are $ D_n = \{ \text{id}, r, r^2, \cdots, r^{n - 1}, s, rs, \cdots, r^{n - 1}s \} $ Which $r$ refers to rotating the polygon by $\frac{2\pi}{n}$ radian, where $s$ refers to reflecting the polygon at a certain direction. Clearly $|D_n| = 2n$, and $D_n = \langle r, s \rangle$, which means $D_n$ is generated by the two generators $r$ and $s$.
