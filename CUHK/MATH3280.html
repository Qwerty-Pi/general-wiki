MATH3280 Introductory Probability

Probability is the art of dealing with uncertainty. We wish to handle probability gracefully using an axiomatic approach, so the notations and concepts can be consistent for over different scenarios.

Probability Space

Consider anything with uncertain outcome, say a coin, a die, or will jesus return before 2027. The sample space \( \Omega \) is defined as the set of all possible outcomes. Additionally, we introduce an event space \( \mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{P}(\Omega) \), where \( E \in \mathcal{F} \) are all the events that we care and handle.

Of course(?), the ordinary set notations apply on events, such as \( E \cup F \), \( E \cap F = EF \), \( \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( \bigcap_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( E \subset F \) and \( E^c \). Actually, we (kinda) require the event space to be closed under the above operations.

Axioms of Probability

What is probability? It is the likelihood of something (event) happens. We can assign a probability, a real number to each of the events, where \( 1 \) means must and \( 0 \) means never. We also expect some possibility must have happened (in the sense that nothing happens is a possibility), while probability of union of disjoint possibilities should be just the sum of them.

We can capture the above intuition by the Axioms of Probability. By definition, probability is a function $P$ that maps from event space \( \mathcal{F} \) to \( \mathbb{R} \), where

  1. For every event \( E \in \mathcal{F} \), \( 0 \le P(E) \le 1 \),
  2. \( P(\Omega) = 1 \) for the whole sample space \( \Omega \),
  3. For countable collection \( \{ E_i \} \) of mutually exclusive events, we have \( P(\bigcup E_i) = \sum P(E_i)\).

We may deduce interesting stuff like the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle from them: \[ P\left(\bigcup E_i\right) = \sum_{S \subset [n]} (-1)^{|S|} \bigcap_{j \in S} E_j \] Which can be proven with induction.

Conditional Probability

How can we concisely state the probability of statements like "if today is Christmas, how likely I am alone"? These kind of conditional probability is denoted as \( P(E \mid F) \), which means "given \( F \), how likely is \( E \)". Assuming \( P(F) \gt 0 \) (otherwise, \( P(E \mid F) \) is meaningless), we have \[ P(E \mid F) = \frac{P(E \cap F)}{P(F)} \] A more generic way is maybe to write \( P(E \cap F) = P(E \mid F) \cdot P(F) \), which always work.

It is easy to get lost in conditional probability. For example, suppose you ask a girl out and she says yes, what is the likelihood that she likes you? It is tempting to over-estimate, but unfortunately \( P(E \mid F) \ne P(F \mid E) \). She might just treat you as a friend!

The above scenario taught us an important lesson: we should be careful when handling conditional probabilities. A famous formula by Bayes reads as conditional odds is bayesian factor times odds: \[ \frac{P(E \mid F)}{P(E^c \mid F)} = \frac{P(F \mid E)}{P(F \mid E^c)} \cdot \frac{P(E)}{P(E^c)} \] So, the mere fact that event \( F \) happens might not mean much to the probability.

Finally, we have the law of total probability: \[ P(F) = \sum_i P(E_i) \cdot P(F \mid E_i) \] given that \( \Omega = \biguplus{ E_i } \).

Independent Events

We intuitively treat unrelated events as independent. For example, what the coin lands on should not dependent on the time, or whether I am betting a million dollars on it (actually maybe depends). Of course, as a math course you cannot merely say you "think" events are independent! We need a formal definition for independence of events. How can we do that?

Formally speaking, we say a collection of events \( \{ E_i \}_{i \in \mathcal{A}} \) is independent if and only if for all finite \( \mathcal{J} \subset \mathcal{A}\), we have \[ P\left(\bigcap_{j \subset \mathcal{J}} E_j\right) = \prod_{j \in \mathcal{J}} P(E_j) \] That is, whatever the way we combine the events together, we still expect the final probability to be computed from the events alone.

When the events are identical and independent, we call the events trials.

Expected Values

Sometimes, we wish to assign values to each of the outcome, say sum of the dots on the dice. We may introduce the concept of random variables as \( X : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \), a real-valued function with sample space as the domain. Actually, events are just a special case of random variables as indicator variable \( X = 1_E \).

Simple Statistics

Given a random variable \( X \), we may want to do some analysis on it. Let's say \( X \) is the random variable for the winning amount of a bet. What do we want to know? First, we want to know the expected value or expectation \[ \mu = \E[X] \] of the bet. When \( \mu \lt 0 \), you should probably reconsider your bet. You might also want to know the variance \[ \sigma^2 = \Var(X) = \E[(X - \mu)^2] \] of the bet, basically the expected value of the deviation from the mean, where \( \sigma = \sqrt{\Var(X)} \ge 0 \) is the standard deviation. If the variance is too big, then it might not be a good news as well.

An interesting fact about expected values is that it is linear in the sense that \[ \E[aX + bY] = a \E[X] + b \E[Y] \] for any random variables. We might as well treat this as an axiom, since we have not formally define \( \E \) yet! However, in reality we always choose \( \E \) such that this linearity of expectations hold. With this, we may prove the well-known identity \( \Var(X) = \E[X^2] - \E[X]^2 \).

Generating Functions and Independence

In fact, we can identify \( X \) by its moments \( \E[X^k] \) where \( k \in \N \). The Moment Generating Function is naturally defined as \[ M(t) = \E[e^{tX}] = 1 + \E[X] \cdot t + \E[X^2] \cdot \frac{t^2}{2} + \cdots \] which contains all the moments of \( X \). Note that it is only well-defined when it is. We shall note that MGFs are bijections, kinda like Fourier or Taylor, although it would not be proven. One may introduce the Cumulant Generating Function \[ K(t) = \log M(t) = \log \E[e^{tX}] \] as well, which generates the mean and variance(!).

Now, we can (somewhat) state the criteria of independence for random variables. That is, \( M_{X, Y}(s, t) = M_X(s) \cdot M_Y(t) \) iff \( X \) and \( Y \) are independent. We have \( \E[X^a Y^b] = \E[X^a] \cdot \E[Y^b] \), and again by Taylor that \[ \E[g(X) \cdot h(Y)] = \E[g(X)] \cdot \E[h(Y)] \] for functions \( g, h : \R \to \R \). Note that \( K_{X + Y}(t) = K_{X}(t) + K_{Y}(t) \), and so \[ \Var(X + Y) = \Var(X) + \Var(Y) \] if \( X \) and \( Y \) are independent. (Note: This definition is slightly sloppy, but it works for the usual case - we are not generalising the notion of random variables, anyways.)

Covariance

The covariance describes the relationship between two random variables, which is defined as \[ \Cov(X, Y) = \E[(X - \E[X])(Y - \E[Y])]\] Which expanding gives \( \Cov(X, Y) = \E[XY] - \E[X] \cdot \E[Y] \). Clearly \( X \) and \( Y \) are independent implies \( \Cov(X, Y) = 0 \), but the converse is not true. Note that \( \Cov \) is symmetric and bilinear while \( \Var(X) = \Cov(X, X) \).

In fact, covariance is just inner product over the space of random variables with zero mean. The "angle" correlation is defined as \[ \rho(X, Y) = \frac{\Cov(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\Var(X) \Var(Y)}}\] Naturally, we have \( -1 \le \rho(X, Y) \le 1 \), and that \( \rho(X, Y) = \pm 1 \iff Y = \pm aX + b \) for \( a \in \mathbb{R}^+ \). We say \( X \) and \( Y \) are positively correlated, negatively correlated and uncorrelated when \( \rho(X, Y) \gt 0 \), \( \rho(X, Y) \lt 0 \) and \( \rho(X, Y) = 0 \) respectively.

Exempler Random Variables

One important distinction to be made here is the discrete or continuous random variables. For some random variable \( X \), its image \( \Omega_X \) is at most countable like \( \N \), while some are uncountable like \( \R \). The random variables of the first case is referred as discrete random variables, while the second case is referred as continuous random variables.

Different treatment for discrete and continuous random variables has to be made, since the summation of uncountable positive values is always unbounded. Instead of summations, we have to use density and integration instead.

Discrete Random Variables

What if the random variable \( X \) is discrete? In such case, we may define the probability mass function, or PMF, as \[ p(a) = P\{ X = a \} \] for all \( a \in \Omega_X \). Obviously for \( S_X = \{ x_i \} \) countable, we have \( p(x_i) \ge 0 \) and \( \sum p(x_i) = 1 \) since the events \( \{ X = x_i \} \) form a partition of \( \Omega \).

We may define the cumulative distribution function, or CDF, as simply \[ F(a) = \sum_{x \le a} p(x) \] Obviously \( F \) is a step function.

What can we infer from the PMF \( p \)? First, the expected value is defined as \[ \E[X] = \sum_{x \in \Omega_X} x \cdot p(x) \] For any function \( g : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \), obviously \( g(X) = g \circ X : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \) is a random variable as well, and so \[ \E[g(X)] = \sum_{x \in \Omega_X} g(x) \cdot p(x) \]

Discrete Examples

There are four examples of random variables that are frequently used: Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson and Geometric.

First, Bernoulli. Suppose the outcome of an experiment is either success or failure, and consider the random variable \( X \) where \( X = 1 \) when success and \( X = 0 \) when failure. If the probability of success is \( p \), then we have the PMF of \( X \) as \[ p(x) = \begin{cases} p & \text{if } x = 1 \\ 1 - p & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases} \] We denote this as \( X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p) \). Obviously we have \( \E[X] = p \) and \( \Var(X) = p(1 - p) \).

Second, Binomial. What if we repeat the Bernoulli trial for \( n \) times, and let the random variable \( X \) refers to the number of successes? Obviously we have \( P\{ X = r \} = \binom{n}{r} p^r (1 - p)^{n - r} \), so the PMF of \( X \) is \[ p(r) = \binom{n}{r} p^r (1 - p)^{n - r} \text{ for } r = 0, \dots, n \] We denote this as \( X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \). With linearity of expected value and variance (of independent events), we have \( \E[X] = np \) and \( \Var(X) = np(1 - p) \).

Third, Poisson. The binomial one is somewhat complicated to compute - turns out we can approximate it pretty well simply when \( n \) is large, \( p \) is small and \( \lambda = np \) moderate. We have \( P\{ X = r \} = \frac{n^{\underline{r}}}{r!} \left( \frac{\lambda}{n - \lambda}\right)^r \left( 1 - \frac{\lambda}{n} \right)^n \approx e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^r}{r!} \), and so we may consider the PMF where \[ p(r) = e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^r}{r!} \text{ for } r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\] as an approximation of \( \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), and denotes as \( \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \). Being a limiting case of binomial, we naturally have \( \E[X] = \Var(X) = \lambda \), which can be shown via simple computations (or, well, generating functions; its gf is \( e^{\lambda(x - 1)} \)).

Finally, Geometric. This random variable is essentially the number of trials needed if we repeat until succeed. If the success probability is \( p \), we clearly have \( P\{ X = r \} = p(1 - p)^{r - 1} \) for \( r \ge 1 \), and so the PMF is \[ p(r) = p(1 - p)^{r - 1} \text{ for } r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\] This is denoted as \( \text{Geometric}(p) \), where \( \E[X] = \frac{1}{p} \) and \( \Var(X) = \frac{1 - p}{p^2} \). (Its gf is \( \frac{px}{1 - (1 - p) x}\).)

Continuous Random Variables

A continuous random variable \( X: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \) is such where for all measurable set \( B \), we have \[ P\{ X \in B \} = \int_B f(x) \, dx \] for some function \( f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \), which is called the probability density function or PDF (reference to physics). With such assumption, by taking \( B = [a, b] \) we have \[ P\{ a \le X \le b \} = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \] and so \( P\{ X = a \} = 0 \) for any \( a \in \mathbb{R} \), which implies the closeness at the endpoints should not affect the probability. Of course, we require \[ 1 = P\{ -\infty \lt X \lt \infty \} = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(x) \, dx \]

Naturally, we define the expected value for a continuous random variable \( X \) as \[ \E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) \, dx \] Analogous to the discrete case, we have \[ \E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) f(x) \, dx \]

Continuous Examples

First, the uniform random variables, where the PDF is \[ f(x) = \frac{1}{b - a} \text{ for } a \le x \le b \] where \( a \lt b \in \mathbb{R} \). Clearly we have \( \E[X] = \frac{a + b}{2} \) and \( \Var(X) = \frac{(b - a)^2}{12} \) from simple computations. We may denote this as \( X \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b) \).

Second, the normal random variable. The PDF of \( X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2) \) is \[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} \exp \frac{-(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\] This often refers as the bell curve, while \( \E[X] = \mu \) and \( \Var(X) = \sigma^2 \). When \( \mu = 0 \) and \( \sigma^2 = 1 \), or \( Z \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1) \) is called the standard normal with PDF \[ f_Z(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \exp \frac{-z^2}{2}\] To show \( \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_Z(z) = 1 \), simply notice that \( \left( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \frac{-z^2}{2} \, dz \right)^2 = \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_{0}^R 2\pi r \exp \frac{-r^2}{2} \, dr = 2 \pi \). The CDF of \( Z \) is often denoted as \( \Phi(z) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z \exp \frac{-y^2}{2} \, dy \). Essentially, when something is distributed normally, then we may refer to the table of \( \Phi \) and get the probability base on how many standard deviations \( \sigma \) it is off from the mean \( \mu \). For instance, for \( X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2) \), then \[ P\{ X \le a \} = \Phi \left( \frac{a - \mu}{\sigma} \right) \] In fact, the normal random variable has a very nice MGF, namely \[ M(t) = \exp \left\{ \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2 t^2}{2} \right\} \]

The DeMoivre-Laplace limit theorem states that as \( n \to \infty \) and for \( X_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), we have \[ \frac{X_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}} \rightarrow \text{Normal}(0, 1) \] Noting \( \E[X] = np \) and \( \Var(X) = np(1 - p) \). We write \( \{ Z_n \} \rightarrow Z \) when for all \( a \lt b \), we have \( P\{ a \le Z_n \le b \} \to P\{ a \le Z \le b \} \). That is, when the number of bernoulli trials becomes large, it starts to behave like a normal distribution. For example, for \( X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), we can approximate \( P\{ X = np \} \) as \( P \{ np - 0.5 \le X \lt np + 0.5 \} \) (continuity correction). With \( \mu = np \) and \( \sigma = \sqrt{np(1 - p)} \), this is \( \Phi\left(\frac{0.5}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}}\right) - \Phi\left(\frac{-0.5}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}}\right) \).

Finally, we introduce the exponential random variables with PDF \[ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \text{ for } x \ge 0 \] Which more relevants to like particle decay. For \( X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda) \), we have \( \E[X^n] = \int_0^\infty x^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = n \int_0^\infty x^{n - 1} e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = \frac{n}{\lambda} \E[X^{n - 1}] = \frac{n!}{\lambda^n} \). Hence, \( \E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda} \) and \( \Var(X) = \E[X^2] - \E[X]^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \).

Joint Distribution Functions

Obviously, we can joint random variables together. For random variables \( X \) and \( Y \), we can always define the joint CDF as \( F(a, b) = P\{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \) for \( -\infty \lt a, b \lt \infty \), where \( \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \) just means \( \{ X \le a \} \cap \{ Y \le b \} \). The marginal CDF for \( X \) and \( Y \) respectively can be computed as \( F_X(a) = F(a, \infty) \) and \( F_Y(b) = F(\infty, b) \). More technically, \( F_X(a) = P \{ X = a \} = P \left( \lim_{b \to \infty} \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \right) = \lim_{b \rightarrow \infty} (P \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} ) = F (a, \infty) \).

When both \( X \) and \( Y \) are discrete, we may define the joint PMF as \( p(x, y) = P \{ X = x, Y = y \} \). The marginal PMF can be obtained in a similar manner as above.

How about joint PDF? Unfortunately, it does not always exists! We say \( X \) and \( Y \) are jointly continuous when there exists a function \( f(x, y) \) such that for any measurable set \( C \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \), we have \[ P\{ (X, Y) \in C \} = \iint_{C} f(x, y) \, dx \, dy \] Which then we may derive that \( f(a, b) = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial a \partial b} F(a, b) \) and the marginal PDF as \( f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y) \, dy \) and \( f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y) \, dx \).

Independent Random Variables

Similar to the case for events (which essentially is binary random variables), we say two random variables \( X \) and \( Y \) are independent whenever for all \( A, B \subset \mathbb{R} \), we have \[ P\{ X \in A, Y \in B \} = P\{ X \in A \} P \{Y \in B \} \] Which is equivalent to \[ P\{ X \le a, Y \le b \} = P\{ X \le a \} P \{ Y \le b \} \] for all \( a, b \in \mathbb{R} \), or equivalently \( F(a, b) = F_X(a) F_Y(b) \). (The proof is non-trivial so is not covered.) In the case of both \( X \) and \( Y \) are discrete, clearly above is equivalent to \( p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y) \) for all \( x, y \), whereas for the case of jointly continuous the above is equivalent to \( f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y) \).

Functions over Random Variables

Given \( X \) and \( Y \) are jointly continuous. How can we find the PDF of \( Z = h(X, Y) \) where \( h: \Omega_{X, Y} \to \R \)? Clearly we have \[ f_Z(z) = \partial_z P\{ h(X, Y) \le z \} \] For example, if \( h(X, Y) = X + Y \), then \[ f_{X + Y}(z) = \int f_{X, Y}(t, z - t) \, dt \] Similarly, if \( (U, V) = h(X, Y) \), then \[ f_{U, V}(u, v) = \partial_{uv} P\{ h(X, Y) \le (u, v)\}\] When \( h \) is \( \mathcal{C}^1 \) diffeomorphism, then clearly the density is simply scaled by the determinant of the Jacobian.

Conditional Distribution Functions

Previously we have discussed about conditional events. How about conditionals on random variables? In the discrete case the definition of conditional PMF is apparent - simply \[ p_{X|Y} (x|y) = P\{ X = x | Y = y\} = \frac{p(x, y)}{p_Y(y)} \] Where we require \( p_Y(y) \gt 0 \). Similarly, the conditional CDF is defined as \[ F_{X|Y}(a|y) = \sum_{x \le a} p_{X|Y}(x|y)\] How about the jointly continuous case? Analogously, we define the conditional PDF as \[ f_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_Y(y)} \] and the conditional CDF as \[ F_{X|Y}(a|y) = \int_{-\infty}^a f_{X|Y}(x|y)\, dx \] Requiring \( f_Y(y) \gt 0 \).

Conditional Expectations

Naturally, we may define the conditional expectation as \[ \E[X | Y = y] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x f_{X|Y} (x|y) \, dx \] In a sense, we may treat conditionals as a reduced sample space restricted to the event \( \{ Y = y \} \), and so formulae for say \( \E[g(X) | Y = y] \) or linearity of expectations remains valid.

Note that \( \E[X|Y] \) is itself a random variable as well, which for each outcome \( s \), \( \E[X|Y](s) = \E[X|Y=Y(s)] \). Trivially, we have \( \E[\E[X|Y]] = \E[X] \), which arguably is just a cooler way to write the expected value version of the Baye's formula.

Trials

Here, we explore some properties of trials - what happens when you repeat the experiment many, many times? It should somehow converges to the mean - but how fast it does?

Sampling

Suppose you sample \( X_1, \dots, X_n \) which are independent and identically distributed random variables, where \( \E[X_i] = \mu \) and \( \Var(X_i) = \sigma^2 \). Turns out we can compute the sample mean \[ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \] and sample variance \[ S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i = 1}^n (X_i - \overline{X})^2 \] and approximate the original: First, we have \( \E[\overline{X}] = \mu \) and \( \Var(\overline{X}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \), both by linearity. Next, \( (n - 1) S^2 = \sum [(X_i - \mu)^2 + (\overline{X} - \mu)^2 - 2 (X_i - \mu) (\overline{X} - \mu)] \), so \( \E[(n - 1) S^2] = n \sigma^2 - n \Var(\overline{X}) = (n - 1) \sigma^2 \) and \( \E[S^2] = \sigma^2 \).

Limit Theorems

Finally, we wish to show that if we repeat the same distribution enough times, the average converges to the mean, and the distribution tends to the normal distribution. We would introduce some estimates and use those to prove results.

First of all, we have the Markov's inequality: For random variable \( X \) which only takes non-negative values, then for \( a \gt 0 \) we have \[ P\{ X \ge a \} \le \frac{\E[X]}{a} \] Letting \( I = [X \ge a] \), then \( \frac{X}{a} \ge I \), and so \( \frac{\E[X]}{a} \ge \E[I] = P\{ X \ge a \} \).

Next, we have Chebyshev's inequality: For random variable \( X \) where \( \E[X] = \mu \) and \( \Var(X) = \sigma^2 \), then for all \( k \gt 0 \), we have \[ P\{ |X - \mu| \ge k \} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2} \] Turns out the proof is very elegant: Note that \( P\{ |X - \mu| \ge k \} = P\{ (X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2 \} \) and that \( \E[(X - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2 \), so ths immediately follows readily from the Markov's inequality. With Markov and Chebyshev, we may bound probabilities (weakly) simply base on the mean and variance. An interesting corollary of Chebyshev is that, if \( \Var(X) = 0 \), then \( X \) must be constant with Chebyshev's inequality.

Now, let's introduce the weak law of large numbers: Let \( \{ X_i \} \) be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables where \( \E[X_i] = \mu \) finite, then for any \( \varepsilon \gt 0 \), as \( n \to \infty \), \[ P \left\{ |\overline{X_n} - \mu| \ge \varepsilon \right\} \rightarrow 0 \] Noting \( \E[\overline{X_n}] = \mu \) and \( \Var(\overline{X_n}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \), and so by Chebyshev we have \[ P \left\{ |\overline{X_n} - \mu| \ge \varepsilon \right\} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2}\]

The strong version of law of large numbers gives us a slightly stronger result: \[ P \left\{ \lim \overline{X_n} = \mu \right\} = 1 \]

Finally, the central limit theorem. Again let \( \{ X_i \} \) be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables where \( \E[X_i] = \mu \) and \( \Var(X_i) = \sigma^2 \). Then as \( n \rightarrow \infty \), \[ \frac{\sum X_i - n \mu}{\sqrt{n} \sigma} \rightarrow \text{Normal}(0, 1)\]

How may we prove the CLT? We assume a lemma that \( M_{Z_n}(t) \rightarrow M_Z(t) \) for all \( t \), then \( F_{Z_t}(t) \rightarrow F_Z(t) \) for all \( t \) where \( F_Z(t) \) is continuous. Assume the moment generating function of \( X_i \) is \( M(t) \) finite, and first consider the case where \( \mu = 0 \) and \( \sigma^2 = 1 \). Then the gf of \( \frac{\sum X_i}{\sqrt{n}} \) is \( [M(t/\sqrt{n})]^n \). Taking \( L(t) = \log M(t) \), we have \( \lim_{n \to \infty} n L(t/\sqrt{n}) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\frac{-t}{2} L'(t/\sqrt{n})}{-n^{-2} \cdot n^{3/2}} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{t L'(t/\sqrt{n})}{2 / \sqrt{n}} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{t^2 L''(t/\sqrt{n})}{2} = \frac{t^2}{2} L''(0) = \frac{t^2}{2} \) And so \( [M(t/\sqrt{n})]^n \rightarrow \exp \frac{t^2}{2} \) as \( n \to \infty \). The general case follows readily by simply writing \( X_i = \sigma Z_i + \mu \), where \( \E[Z_i] = 0 \) and \( \Var(Z_i) = 1 \).

More generally, for any sequence of independent random variables where \( \E[X_i] = \mu_i \) and \( \Var(X_i) = \sigma_i^2 \), \( X_i \) is uniformly bounded and \( \sum \sigma_i^2 \rightarrow \infty \), then we have as \( n \to \infty \), \[ P\left\{ \frac{\sum (X_i - \mu_i)}{\sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2} } \le a \right\} \rightarrow \Phi(a) \]
