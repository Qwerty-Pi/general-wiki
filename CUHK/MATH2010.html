MATH2010 Advanced Calculus I

Single-variable calculus are somewhat more simple. How about vector-valued multi-variable functions? Can we study rate of change, finding maximum / minimum and do approximations, say for function $f: \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ with $f(x, y, z) = (x + y, e^x)$? Well, we have to first define what a vector is in the Euclidean Space.

Euclidean Space

The Euclidean Space $\R^n = \R \times \R \times \cdots \times \R = \{ (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) : x_i \in \R \}$ is the set of points (or vectors) in the $n$-dimensional space. We also call the elements of $\R$ scalars. For points $A, B \in \R^n$, the vector $\overrightarrow{AB}$ is the vector from $A$ to $B$, where $\big\Vert \overrightarrow{AB} \big\Vert$ is the magnitude or norm of $\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Vector Operations

We may simply define vector operations such as equality, addition and scalar multiplication just normally. How to define length, area and volume in terms of vectors?

To incorporate the idea of length, we define dot product $a \cdot b = \sum a_i \cdot b_i$ for $a, b \in \R^n$. The euclidean norm of vector $a$ is just $\Vert a \Vert = \sqrt{a \cdot a}$. Alternatively, it can be represent as $\langle a, b \rangle$. We can derive two inequalities, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, $| a \cdot b | \le \Vert a \Vert \cdot \Vert b \Vert$ and the triangle inequality, $\Vert a + b \Vert \le \Vert a \Vert + \Vert b \Vert$, which can both be easily proven.

How about areas and volumes? We just handle the case for $\R^3$ here, because higher dimension area or volume is hard to define. We may introduce the cross product $a \times b$, defined as $$ \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}$$ Clearly $\times$ is bilinear and $b \times a = - a \times b$ directly from the properties of determinant. Similarly, we may define the triple product $a \cdot (b \times c)$ as $$ \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}$$ Also rely on the notion of determinant.

From elementary geometry we can deduce that if the inclined angle between $a$ and $b$ is $\theta$, then $a \cdot b = \Vert a \Vert \Vert b \Vert \cos \theta$ and $\Vert a \times b \Vert = \Vert a \Vert \Vert b \Vert \sin \theta$, so cross product $\Vert a \times b \Vert$ is the area of the parallelogram formed by $a$ and $b$. Note $a \times b \perp a, b$, so the triple product $(a \times b) \cdot c$ is actually the signed volume of the parallelopipe formed by $a$, $b$ and $c$.

Linear Objects

In the 2D plane everything is simple: We can either define lines in the form of linear equations $ax + by = c$ for $a, b, c \in \R$, or in parametric form $a + bt$ for $a, b \in \R^n$ and $t \in \R$. How can we translate those ideas into higher dimensions?

Actually it is pretty simple as well. We may define a hyperplane as set of points in the form $\{ x \in \R^n : a \cdot x = b \}$ where $a \in \R^n \setminus \{ 0 \}$ and $b \in \R$. Then, a linear object can be defined as intersection of hyperplanes. Linear algebra wise, then the dimension of the hyperplane is simply $n - k$ where $k$ is the rank of $A$ as in $A \cdot x = b$. Alternatively, it can also be represented similarly in the parametric form $a + b_1 t_1 + \cdots + b_k t_k$ with $\{ b_i \}$ being linear independent and $t_i \in \R$, representing a linear object of dimension $k$.

Curves

How about curves? We want to have a formal definition that encodes the intuitive definition of "put down a pen, move the pen, lift up the pen". Formally, a curve is a continuous function $x: I \rightarrow \R^n$ where $I \subset \R$ is interval. A curve $x: [a, b] \rightarrow \R^n$ is closed if $x(a) = x(b)$, and simple if $x(t_1) \ne x(t_2)$ for all $t_1 \lt t_2$ except at endpoints.

It is natural for us to extend the definition of limit and derivative from the single-variable cases as $\lim_{t \rightarrow a} x(t) = (\lim_{t \rightarrow a} x_1(t), \dots, \lim_{t \rightarrow a} x_n(t))$ and $x'(t) = \lim_{h \rightarrow 0} \frac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h}$. We call $x'(t)$ the tangent vector of $x(t)$. Clearly $x'$ inherits all the single-variable rules.

How about the arclength of $\mathcal{C}^1$ (continuous-differentiable) curve $x$ for $a \le t \le b$? Intuitively, we may define the arclength $s$ as $\sup_{\{t_i\}} \Vert x(t_i) - x(t_{i - 1}) \Vert$, the supremum of the length of piecewise approximation, and equivalently $\int_{a}^b \Vert x'(t) \Vert \, dt$, noting $x(t_i) - x(t_{i - 1}) = x'(\xi) (t_i - t_{i - 1})$ for some $\xi \in (t_{i - 1}, t_i)$ by mean-value theorem.

Coordinate Systems

Other than the old-boring rectangular coordinates, sometimes we will handle points on circles, cylinder, spheres etc. How should we encode points in these coordinate systems?

First of all, we have the polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in $\R^2$, where $$\begin{cases} x = r \cos \theta \\ y = r \sin \theta \end{cases} \hspace{10mm} \begin{cases} r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\ \theta = \arctan \frac{y}{x} \end{cases}$$ Conventionally, we assume $r \in [0, \infty)$ and $\theta \in \R$. Intuitively, $r$ is the distance from the point to the origin while $\theta$ is the inclined angle.

Second, we have the cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ in $\R^3$, which is simply adding the third dimension, z-axis, to the polar coordinates.

A more complicated one is the spherical coordinates $(\rho, \theta, \phi)$, where $$\begin{cases} x = \rho \sin \phi \cos \theta \\ y = \rho \sin \phi \sin \theta \\ z = \rho \cos \phi \end{cases} \hspace{10mm} \begin{cases} \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} \\ \theta = \arctan \frac{y}{x} \\ \phi = \arccos \frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}\end{cases}$$ Where essentially $\rho$ is the distance to origin, $\theta \in \R$ is the latitude and $\phi \in [0, \pi]$ is the longitude. This is especially useful when, well, handling spherical stuff.

Topology in Euclidean Space

In the euclidean space $\R^n$, we can define an open ball as $$B_\varepsilon(x_0) = \{ x \in \R^n : \Vert x - x_0 \Vert \lt \varepsilon \}$$ And a closed ball as $$\overline{B_\varepsilon(x_0)} = \{ x \in \R^n : \Vert x - x_0 \Vert \le \varepsilon \}$$ For $S \subset \R^n$, we define the interior of $S$ as $$ \text{Int}(S) := \{ x \in \R^n : B_\varepsilon(x) \subset S \text{ for some } \varepsilon \gt 0 \} $$ Similarly, we define the exterior of $S$ as $$ \text{Ext}(S) := \{ x \in \R^n : B_\varepsilon(x) \subset \R^n \setminus S \text{ for some } \varepsilon \gt 0 \} $$ Finally, the boundary of $S$ as $$ \partial S := \R^n \setminus (\text{Int}(S) \cup \text{Ext}(S))$$

Specifically, we call a set open when $S = \text{Int}(S)$, and closed when $S = \text{Int}(S) \cup \partial S$. We also define the closure of $S$ as $S \cup \partial S$. Note that open and closed are not of opposite definitions; rather these are two related properties. Specially, $\R^n$ and $\varnothing$ are both open and closed, while set like $\mathbb{Q}$ are neither open or close in $\R$.

Finally, we also say $S \subset \R^n$ is bounded whenever there exists an $M$ where $S \subset B_M(0)$, and unbounded otherwise; And a set $S$ is connected whenever for all $x, y \in S$ there exists a curve connecting $x$ and $y$, and disconnected otherwise. The Jordan Curve Theorem states that a simple closed curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$ partition the region into two connected components, one bounded and one unbounded.
