PHYS3021 Quantum Mechanics I

Foundations

Since I know nothing about physics, here are some well-known related facts.

Electromagnetic Waves

In a region without electric charges or currents, the electric or magnetic field ($E, B : \R^3 \times \R \to \C$) satisfies the Maxwell equation where \( \Psi = E \) or \( B \) as \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \Psi = 0 \] Assuming \( F \) is separable, i.e., we can write \( \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) \times f(t) \), then \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial_t^2 f}{f} = \frac{\nabla^2 \psi}{\psi} \] with each sides being constants. Naturally, we have \( f(t) = \exp i (\omega t) \) and \( \psi(r) = \exp i (k \cdot r) \) for some constants \( \omega \) and \( k \). Combine together, we have the wave equation \[ \Psi(r, t) = A \exp i (k \cdot r - \omega t)\]

In words, \( \Psi \) is a sinusodial propagating wave with wave vector \( k \), the change in phase due to position, or the propagation direction of the wave; and angular frequency \( \omega \), the change in phase due to time. For EM waves, the dispersion relation is \( \omega = c \cdot |k| \), since EM waves travel at light speed \( c \). In practice, we assume that wave of any (valid) shape can be written as a superposition (sum) of multiple seperable plane waves, which kinda solves the general case.

We recall other concepts in waves. The frequency is \( f = \frac{\omega}{2 \pi} \), the number of complete phase revolutions per unit time. The wavelength is defined as \( \lambda = \frac{2 \pi}{|k|} \), the length of a complete phase. For EM waves, we have \( c = f \cdot \lambda \). Finally, the wavenumber (more in chemsitry) is defined as \( \tilde \nu = \frac{1}{\lambda} \), the reciprocal of wavelength.

Blackbody Radiation and Quantised Energy

Suppose we have a physical object, which can absorbs and emits radiations. We idealise a blackbody as a perfect absorber that absorbs all incoming light (EM waves) and reflect nothing from its surface, then the radiant energy we measured from the blackbody only come from itself. Turns out that for a blackbody, the radiant energy desnity over wavelengths only depends on the temperature.

Classically, we would expect an infrared catastrophe, which the energy density grows uncontrollably as wavelength decreases, which doesn't match the experimental results. Max Planck proposes the idea which we should quantise the energy \( E = nhf \) where \( n \in \N \) and \( h \) is the Planck's constant, which derives the more-accurate Planck's formula for radiant energy density for blackbody as \[ u(f) \, df = 2 \times \frac{4 \pi f^2 \, df}{c^3} \times \frac{hf}{e^{hf/(k_B T)} - 1}\] where the first two terms correspond to the number of modes with frequency within \( [f, f + df] \), and the last term correspond to the average energy per mode.

Photon and Matter Waves

Einstein proposes that light exists in photons, as particles. For a photon of frequency \( f \) and wavevector \( k \), it carries the quantised energy \( E = hf = \hbar \omega \) and momentum \( p = \hbar k \). Compton use X-ray to scatter electrons, proving that light does have momentum. Boltzmann said that the probability of state with energy \( E_n = nhf \) is \( p_n \propto \exp -\frac{E_n}{k_B T} \) and we trust him, so the average number of photons with frequency \( f \) is, namely the Bose-Einstein statistics, \[ \langle n_f \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{hf/(k_B T)} - 1}\]

Afterwards, de Brogile proposes that particles exists as matter waves. For a particle of (relativistic) energy \( E \) and momentum \( p \), its matter wave has \( f = E / h \) and \( \lambda = h / p \). The phase velocity \( v_{ph} = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{c^2}{v} \), where the group velocity \( v_{g} = \partial_k \omega = v \). It is really the group velocity that carries information.

Dimensional Analysis

In physics, we have dimension for every quantity. As the old saying goes, you cannot add apples to oranges, we can only add quantities in the same dimensions together. It is often useful for sanity check of whether the computation result make senses.

For a physical quantity \( X \), we use \( [X] \) to represent its dimension. The basic units include mass \( [m] = M \), length \( [l] = L \), time \( [t] = T \) (and four more). We can derive the dimensions of other physical quantity base on them. For example, planck's quantised action \( [\hbar] = ML^2T^{-1} \), angular frequency \( [\omega] = T^{-1} \), momentum \( [p] = MLT^{-1} \) and energy \( [E] = ML^2T^{-2} \). Basically, the only binary operation we can do on non-dimensionless physical quantities is just multiply them together, for example \( [h \omega] = [h][\omega] = ML^2T^{-2} = [E] \).

Schrodinger's Equation

Classically, in Newtonian mechanics we have \( F = ma \), where the force governs the movement of a particle. In a similar manner, the (non-relativistic) Schrodinger's Equation governs the evolution of a quantum wave as \[ i \hbar \partial_t \Psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \Psi + V \Psi \] where \( V : \R^3 \to \R \) is the potential and \( \Psi : \R^3 \times \R \to \C \) is the quantum wave. Effectively, the evolution of \( \Psi \) depends on a "kinetic" component \( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \) and a "potential" component \( V \).

Schrodinger initially interprets \( \Psi \) as the charge density (in e.g. hydrogen atom). Later on, Born interpreted \( \Psi \) as probability density, where the density is \( |\Psi|^2 \) or \( \Psi^* \Psi \). As a PDF, the amplitude of quantum waves doesn't matter (unlike EM waves), and we expect \( \Psi \) to be normalised where \( \int |\Psi|^2 dV = 1 \). Formally, this means \( \Psi \) is a \( L^2 \) (square-integrable) function.

Time-Independent Schrodinger

Since Schodinger is a linear PDE, its solution forms a vector space. If we assume \( \Psi \) is seperable where \( \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) \times f(t) \), then \[ i \hbar \frac{\partial_t f}{f} = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\nabla^2 \psi}{\psi} + V \] since LHS only depends on time and RHS only depends on position, both sides equal to a constant(!) say \( E \) (energy), and \[ \partial_t f = \frac{E}{\hbar i} f \qquad\qquad \left( - \frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m} + V \right) \psi = E \psi \] The first equation simply implies \( f = \exp (-i\omega t) \). The second equation is more interesting, which requires knowing \( V \) and the boundary conditions to be solved.

Fourier Transform

Now, we want to write quantum wave \( \psi \) into the superposition of plane waves, namely Fourier Transform as \[ \psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tilde \psi (k) e^{ikx} \, dk \] Turns out its inverse is very simple: simply take \[ \tilde \psi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x) e^{-ikx} \, dx \] We treat \( \psi(x) \) as a wave function in the position space, and \( \tilde \psi(k) \) as a wave function in the \( k \)-space or the momentum space (since \( p = \hbar k \)).

Define the inner product between wave functions as \[ \ip{\psi}{\varphi} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^*(x) \varphi(x) \, dx \] How to compute \( \ip{e^{ikx}}{e^{iqx}} \)? Mathematically it always diverge, but physically we can assume a infinitesimal decay of \( e^{-\eta|x|} \) where \( \eta \to 0 \). It is easy to see \[ \ip{e^{ikx}}{e^{iqx}} = \begin{cases} \infty & \text{ if } k = q \\ 0 & \text{ if } k \ne q \end{cases}\] which is a "generalised" function. We can say a bit more - note that \[ \iint e^{ikx - \eta |x|} \, dx \, dk = \int \frac{2 \eta}{\eta^2 + k^2} \, dk = 2 \pi \] the "magnitude of the infinity" is \( 2 \pi \). By introducing the Dirac delta \( \delta \) where \( \delta(x) = \infty \) when \( x = 0 \), \( \delta(x) = 0 \) when \( x \ne 0 \) and \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x) \, dx = 1 \), we can write \( \ip{e^{ikx}}{e^{iqx}} = 2 \pi \delta(q - k) \). Actually this is just a formulation for the concept of point mass.

We may use \( \delta \) to prove the inverse relation of Fourier Transform: \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int \tilde \psi(k) e^{ikx} \, dk = \int \psi(y) \delta(x - y) \, dy = \psi(x) \]

Operators

Operators are transformation between functions. For example, we denote \( \hat x \) as the operator \( \psi \mapsto x \psi \), and \( \hat p_x \) as the operator \( \psi \mapsto -i \hbar \partial_x \psi \). Similarly, we can define \( \hat y = y, \hat z = z, \hat p_y = -i \hbar \partial_y, \hat p_z = -i \hbar \partial_z \) and \( \hat p = -i \hbar \nabla \). In operator form, we can write the Hamiltonian operator as \( \hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \hat V \), and Schrodinger's Equation as \( -i \hbar \partial_t \Psi = \hat H \Psi \).

Define the commutator \( [\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A \hat B - \hat B \hat A \). Clearly the commutator is bilinear and skew-symmetric. A very important relation is \( [\hat x, \hat p] = i \hbar \). By abusing Taylor, we also have \( [\hat p, \hat V(x)] = -i \hbar V'(x) \).

We can define the (vector) angular momentum as \( \hat L = \hat r \times \hat p \). Interestingly, we have \( [\hat L, \hat L] = i \hbar \hat L \). The total (scalar) angular momentum is defined as \( \hat L^2 = \hat L_x^2 + \hat L_y^2 + \hat L_z^2 \). Under the cylindrical coordinates \( (\rho, \phi, z) \), we have the nice relation \( \hat L_z = \frac{\hbar}{i} \partial_\phi \).

Hermitian Operators

Here, we shall introduce an important concept: we say an operator \( \hat V \) is Hermitian or self-adjoint when \( \ip{\hat V \psi}{\varphi} = \ip{\psi}{\hat V \varphi} \) for all wave functions \( \psi \) and \( \varphi \). Easy to verify that \( \hat x \) and \( \hat p \) are both Hermitian. In fact, \( \hat H \) is Hermitian as well, since \( \ip{\hat H \psi}{\varphi} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \ip{\nabla^2 \psi}{\varphi} + \ip{V \psi}{\varphi} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \ip{\psi}{\nabla^2 \varphi} + \ip{\psi}{V \varphi} = \ip{\psi}{\hat H \varphi} \), where we used integration by-parts and assumed \( V \) is real.

What so special about Hermitian operators? It is mostly concerning eigenvectors. First, eigenvalues \( E \) must be real since \( E^* \ip{\psi}{\psi} = \ip{\hat H \psi}{\psi} = \ip{\psi}{\hat H \psi} = E \ip{\psi}{\psi} \). Eigenvectors correspond to different eigenvalues are always orthogonal as well, since \( E_\psi \ip{\psi}{\varphi} = \ip{\hat H \psi}{\varphi} = \ip{\psi}{\hat H \varphi} = E_\varphi \ip{\psi}{\varphi} \), forcing \( \ip{\psi}{\varphi} = 0 \). According to some generalisation of the spectral theorem (which we won't and can't prove here), we can find a complete orthogonal eigenbasis for the underlying space.

Time-Dependent Schrodinger

Previously, we assume \( \Psi \) is separable and is time-independent. How to solve the general case for \( i \hbar \partial_t \Psi = \hat H \Psi \)? Since the Hamiltonian \( \hat H \) is Hermitian, \( \Psi \) is simply a superposition of the eigenstates \( \psi \) satisfying \( \hat H \psi = E \psi \)! Specifically, if \( \Psi(r, 0) = \sum c_n \psi_n \), then \( \Psi(r, t) = \sum c_n \psi_n e^{-iE_n t / \hbar} \). By playing with notations, we can write in a cool way: let \( \hat U(t) = e^{-i \hat H t / \hbar} \), then \( \Psi(t) = \hat U(t) \Psi(0) \).

Probability Current and Continuity Equation

An important idea in physics is conservation and continuity: the total quantity is conserved, while the quantity can only move continuously. In general, the continuity equation is of the form \[ \partial_t \rho + \nabla \cdot j = 0 \] where \( \rho \) refers to the density and \( j \) refers to the flux. This basically means that the rate of change in density \( \partial_t \rho \) is the negation of the rate of outward flow \( \nabla \cdot j \).

What if \( \rho \) refers to the probability density \( |\Psi|^2 \)? By Schrodinger, we have \( \partial_t \rho = \Psi^* \frac{\hat H}{i \hbar}\Psi + \text{c.c.} = \Psi^* [ -\frac{\hbar}{2mi} \nabla^2 + \frac{V}{i \hbar} ] \Psi + \text{c.c.} = -\frac{\hbar}{2mi} \nabla \cdot (\Psi^* \nabla \Psi - \Psi \nabla \Psi^*) \). Hence, by define the probability current \[ j = \frac{\hbar}{2mi} (\Psi^* \nabla \Psi - \Psi \nabla \Psi^*) \] the continuity equation holds and the probability is always conserved.

If we write \( \Psi = f e^{i \phi} \) where amplitude \( f \) and phase \( \phi \) are real functions, then \( j = \frac{\hbar}{m} \Im(\Psi^* \nabla \Psi) = \frac{\hbar}{m} \rho \nabla \phi \). So, in a sense, the probability current can be interpreted as the probability density times velocity.

Quantum Measurement

Quantum waves are probabilistic in nature. What happens when we take a measurement on its position or its momentum? By preparing an ensemble (random samples) of a quantum wave and repeatedly take measurement, we can approximate the statistics of the measurement.

Statistics

We denote \( \langle X \rangle \) as the expectation of the "physical property" \( X \). For example, the expectation of position \( \langle x \rangle \) is \[ \langle x \rangle = \int \psi^*(x) \, x \, \psi(x) \, dx \] and the expectation of momentum \( \langle p \rangle \) is \[ \langle p \rangle = \int \tilde \psi^*(k) \, (\hbar k) \, \tilde \psi(k) \, dk \] To simply the notations a bit, we use \( \tip{\psi_1}{X}{\psi_2} \) to denote \( \int \psi_1^*(x) \, X \, \psi_2(x) \, dx \). Magically, \( \tip{\tilde \psi}{p}{\tilde \psi} = \ipx{\tilde \psi}{\ipx{ \hbar i \partial_x e^{ikx}}{\psi}} = \ipx{\tilde \psi}{\ipx{e^{ikx}}{\frac{\hbar}{i} \partial_x \psi}} = \tip{\psi}{\frac{\hbar}{i} \partial_x}{\psi} \). Therefore, we can equivalently represent \( \langle p \rangle \) in the position space as \( \langle \frac{\hbar}{i} \partial_x \rangle \) as well. More generally, we have \( \langle V(x, p) \rangle = \tip{\psi}{V\left(x, \frac{\hbar}{i} \partial_x \right)}{\psi} \).

We can compute the variance and standard variations for position and momentum as \( (\Delta x)^2 = \langle x^2 \rangle - \langle x \rangle^2 \) and \( (\Delta p)^2 = \langle p^2 \rangle - \langle p \rangle^2 \). By absolutely abusing notations, we have \[ \ip{x}{x} \cdot \ip{p}{p} \ge |\ip{x}{p}|^2 \ge \frac{1}{4}[\hat x, \hat p]^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \] and the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle \[ \Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \]

Ehrenfest Theorem

A natural question is - how does the expectation of an operator \( \hat A \) evolves over time? We have \[ \frac{d}{dt} \langle \hat A \rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar} \langle [\hat H, \hat A]\rangle + \langle \partial_t \hat A \rangle\] and so \( \frac{d}{dt} \langle \hat x \rangle = \frac{1}{m} \langle \hat p \rangle \) and \( \frac{d}{dt} \langle \hat p \rangle = -\langle V'(x) \rangle \). This somewhat resembles the classical mechanics, but once \( V(x) \) is of higher order than quadratic, then the term of quantum fluctuation \( \langle V'(x) \rangle - V'(\langle x \rangle) \) appears, and so quantum mechanics is not equivalent to the classical mechanics at all.

Postulates

We claim that all classical "physical property" or observable can be derived from position and momentum. To measure an observable (operator) \( \hat A \), we require \( \hat A \) to be both linear and Hermitian. These two properties are important, since we implicitly assume them all the times.

If the quantum state is \( \Psi \), then we can measure the eigenvalue \( a_n \) with probability \( \ip{\Psi_n}{\Psi} \) given \( \hat A \Psi_n = a_n \Psi_n \), and the state \( \Psi \) collapses (changes) to \( \Psi_n \) theoretically. Practically, the quantum state could be destroyed, and the state would evolve again afterwards as well.

Zoology of Schrodinger in 1D

Okay, enough theory. Now, we want to solve actual cases for Schrodinger's Equation.

Free Particles

The simplest case is when \( V = 0 \), which means the particle is free, and we have hamiltonian \( \hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \). Solving \( \hat H \psi = E \psi \), we have \( \psi = e^{i k \cdot r} \) where \( \Vert k \Vert = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar} \). In other words, \[ E_k = \frac{\hbar^2 |k|^2}{2m} \] Note that there is two solutions in 1D and infinitely many in higher dimensions.

Mathematically we can allow \( E \le 0 \) or even complex \( E \), but for \( \psi \) to be normalisable, we cannot allow the amplitude of \( \psi \) to shoot up exponentially as \( |r| \to \infty \). This is one of the boundary conditions that we need to take into account.

Note that the eigenstates \( \psi = e^{i k \cdot r} \) are not normalisable on their own as well! We call them continuum states. However, since they do not shoot up exponentially, a superposition of them can actually produce a valid quantum wave. Indeed, we can use Fourier Transform to write arbitrary quantum waves into a superposition of plane waves.

Infinite Well

A slightly more complicated example is the infinite well. Consider the potential well \[ V(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{ if } x \in [0, a] \\ \infty & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases} \] Then for \( x \) outside of \( [0, a] \) we obviously have \( \psi(x) = 0 \). How about \( x \in [0, a] \)? Clearly, we have \( \psi(x) = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx} \) for complex \( A \) and \( B \). Note that we also have the boundary conditions that \( \psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0 \), since \( \psi \) is continuous so that \( \nabla^2 \psi \) exists. By writing \[ M_{BC} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ e^{ikx} & e^{-ikx} \end{pmatrix} \] and require \( M_{BC} \) to be singular, we forces \( k = \frac{n \pi}{a} \) for some \( n \in \Z \). It is easy to see all the eigenstates \[ \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin \frac{n\pi x}{a} \] with eigenenergy \[ E_n = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \cdot \frac{n^2 \pi^2}{a^2} \] are normalisable, and are called bounded states.

How does \( \langle x \rangle \) evolves with time? For an eigenstate \( \psi_n \), we have \( \langle x \rangle = \frac{a}{2} \) being stationary. However, when \( \psi \) is a superposition say \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\psi_n + \psi_m) \), then there is an additional oscillating dipole \[ \int_0^a \frac{2}{a} \sin \frac{n \pi x}{a} \sin \frac{m \pi x}{a} \cos \frac{E_n - E_m}{\hbar} \, dx \] whenever \( n \) and \( m \) has the same parity. We can express this oscillating component using the Heisenberg matrix as \( x_{n, m} \cos \omega_{n, m} \) where \( x_{n, m} = \tip{\psi_n}{\hat x}{\psi_m} \) and \( \omega_{n, m} = \frac{E_n - E_m}{\hbar} \).

Harmonic Oscillator
