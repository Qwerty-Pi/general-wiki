MATH2020 Advanced Calculus II

Another thing in calculus that concerns us is integration. In higher dimensional Euclidean space, how can we define stuff like volumes and surface areas, or integral over a volume / surface rigourously?

Riemann Integral

Consider a bounded function $f: \Omega \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$. How shall we define the integral $\int_{\Omega} f \, dV$, intuitively "summing" $f$ over some subset $\Omega$? Well, it is complicated to consider arbirary "shaped" $\Omega$, so let's first focus on the simple case - rectangles.

Rectangle and Partition

We define a rectangle $R$ over $\R^n$ as a set of the form $[a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2] \times \cdots \times [a_n, b_n]$. The volume of a rectangle is intuitively defined as $\text{Vol}(R) = \prod_i (b_i - a_i)$. Let's say we subdivide each axis $[a_i, b_i]$ by $a_i = x_{i,0} \lt x_{i,1} \lt \cdots \lt x_{i,k_i} = b_i$, then we have a partition $\P$ of rectangle $R$. Note that partitions must be finite.

For two partitions $\P_1$ and $\P_2$ of the same rectangle $R$, we say $\P_1$ is a refinement of $\P_2$ whenever $\P_1$ contains all the subdividing points of $\P_2$, or alternatively, for every $Q_1 \in \P_1$, there exists $Q_2 \in \P_2$ such that $Q_1 \subset Q_2$. For any two partitions, we can also consider their common refinement by considering the union of the subdividing points. Easy to see that one may treat the collection of all partitions as a poset.

Riemann Integrable over Rectangles

Let $f : R \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$ be a bounded function defined on rectangle $R$. The core idea of integration is to cut the domain into small rectangles - let's say $\P$ is a partition of $R$. Then we can estimate the lower sum by $$\L(f, \P) = \sum_{Q \in \P} \inf_{x \in Q} f(x) \cdot \text{Vol}(Q) $$ And the upper sum by $$ \U(f, \P) = \sum_{Q \in \P} \sup_{x \in Q} f(x) \cdot \text{Vol}(Q) $$ Which essentially summing the extreme values over the subrectangles, which infinum and supremum exists since we assumed boundedness.

We can deduce quite some inequalities from definitions and facts about infinum and supremum. First, we have $\L(f, \P) \le \U(f, \P)$ for any partition $\P$, since infinum is at most as large as supremum. Next, if $\P'$ is a refinement of $\P$, then $\L(f, \P) \le \L(f, \P')$ and $\U(f, \P') \le \U(f, \P)$, since $\inf_{x \in Q} f(x) \le \inf_{x \in Q'} f(x)$ for $Q \supset Q'$. Finally, for any two partitions $\P$ and $\P'$ of $R$, we have $\L(f, \P) \le \U(f, \P')$, since for the common refinement $\P''$ of $\P$ and $\P'$ we have $$ \L(f, \P) \le \L(f, \P'') \le \U(f, \P'') \le \U(f, \P') $$ And so $ \sup_\P \L(f, \P) \le \inf_\P \U(f, \P) $.

Thus, we can define the lower integral as $$ \underline{\int}_R f \, dV = \sup_{\P} \L(f, \P) $$ And the upper integral as $$ \overline{\int}_R f \, dV = \inf_{\P} \U(f, \P) $$ Which is the limits of the lower sum and upper sum respectively in the analytical sense. We say $f$ is Riemann integrable when $\underline{\int}_R f \, dV = \overline{\int}_R f \, dV$, and Riemann integral $\int_R f \, dV$ is defined to the same value. Simply analysis gives us the alternative Riemann condition for deiciding whether a function $f$ is integrable, which is whenever for each $\varepsilon \gt 0$, there exists a partition $\P$ such that $\U(f, \P) - \L(f, \P) \lt \varepsilon$.

Integrating Continuous Functions

What if we additionally require $f$ being continuous? Turns out that is sufficient for us to claim that $\int_R f \, dV$ is well-defined. Since $R$ is compact, we can cover $R$ with finite number of cubes $C$ where $\sup_{x \in C} f(x) - \inf_{x \in C} f(x) \lt \varepsilon$, which implies $f$ is integrable by riemann condition by considering the partition induced by the cubes.

With similar logic, if the set of discontinuity $D$ is not too "big", then $f$ is still reimann integrable. For example, if $D$ is just a singleton, then we can simply "encircle" the point with an infinitesimal rectangle, then the discontinuity would only affects a small volume. So the question now becomes - can we have some effective definitions for us to decide the set of discontinuity is not too "big"?

Content Zero and Measure Zero

Consider a set $A \subset \R^n$. We say $A$ is of content zero if foreach $\varepsilon \gt 0$, there exists finite set of rectangles $\{ R_i \}$ that covers $A$ where $\sum \text{Vol}(R_i) \lt \varepsilon$, and of measure zero if countably infinite set of rectangles allowed. (Note that sum of uncountable positive reals is always unbounded.)

Clearly content zero implies measure zero, while the union of a countable collection of measure zero sets stays measure zero by taking $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon \cdot 2^{-n}$ on the $n$-th set. Consider the boundary $\partial A$ (can be approached from both outside and inside $A$), then if $A$ is content zero, $\partial A$ is content zero as well by slightly enlarging the rectangles. However, such does not hold for measure zero, since say $A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is measure zero but $\partial A = [0, 1]$ is not of measure zero.

In fact, a bounded function $f$ is Riemann integrable over rectangle $R$ iff its set of discontinuity is measure zero.

Riemann Integrable over Arbitrary Subset

Now we generalise the definition to $f: \Omega \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$ as a bounded function. To reuse the definition we have for rectangles, we define the extension $\overline{f}: R \rightarrow \R$ where $\overline{f}(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in \Omega$ and $\overline{f}(x) = 0$ for all $x \in R \setminus \Omega$, such that $\Omega \subset R$ any rectangle. Clearly the choice of rectangle does not matter. Now, we simply have $\int_{\Omega} f \, dV = \int_R \overline{f} \, dV$.
