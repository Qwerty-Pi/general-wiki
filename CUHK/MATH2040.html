MATH2040 Linear Algebra II

Vector Space

To discuss vector space, we first need a notion of field. A field $F$ is an abstraction over $\mathbb{R}$, which is an algebraic structure that supports binary operations $+$ (addition) and $\cdot$ (multiplication) as well as their inverses, subtraction and division (dividing non-zero elements). The distributive law also holds for field, that is, $\cdot$ is distributive over $+$.

A vector space is a set of vectors $\V$ over scalar field $F$, equipped with $+: \V \times \V \rightarrow \V$ and $\cdot: F \times \V \rightarrow \V$ such that $(\vec{x}, \vec{y}) \mapsto \vec{x} + \vec{y}$ and $(a, \vec{x}) \mapsto a \vec{x}$ where $\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in \V$, $a \in F$, where the arrow above $x$ is used to denote vectors (omitted when clear). Similar to other algebraic structures, the reason why we introduced this definition is for the sake of abstraction. In fact, vector space generalises the concept of sequences or euclidean space. Examples of vector space includes $F^n$, $M_{m \times n}(F)$ ($m \times n$ matrices), $P(F)$ (polynomials), $F^{\infty}$ (infinite sequences), $\text{Sym}_{n \times n}(F)$ (symmetric matrices) and $\mathcal{F}(S, F)$ (functions from $S$ to $F$).

Vector Subspace

The vector space $\W \subset \V$ is called a subspace of $\V$ whenever $0_\V \in \W$ and is closed under $+$ and $\cdot$. Note that any intersection of subspaces $\bigcap_{\W \in S} \W \subset \V$ is a subspace as well straight from definition.

For subspaces $\U, \W \subset \V$, the sum of $\U$ and $\W$ is defined as $\U + \W = \{ x + y : x \in \U, y \in \W \}$. We denote $\V = \U \oplus \W$ in which $\V$ is the direct sum of $\U$ and $\W$ when $\V = \U + \W$ and $\U \cap \W = \{ 0 \}$, which specially each $v \in \V$ can be uniquely written as $u + w$ with $u \in \U$ and $w \in \W$. Direct sum of multiple subspaces is written as $\U_1 \oplus \U_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \U_k$. Note that all the above creates new vector subspaces. We can further define projection $\Pi: \U \oplus \W \rightarrow \U$ with $u + w \mapsto u$, which intuitively projects vector in $\U \oplus \W$ onto $\U$.

Linear Independence, Span and Basis

One thing concerning us is - how to represent vector spaces when they contain way too many elements? We want to represent it as a basis set of vectors, which every elements of the vector space can be generated from it uniquely.

Consider a set of vectors $S$. We call $v$ as a linear combination of $S$ when $v = \sum_k a_k \vec{u_k}$ for some $a_k \in F$ and $\vec{u_k} \in S$. Note that the summation is finite, since infinite summation is not well-defined in the abstract sense without convergence! A set $S$ is called linearly independent when $\vec{v} = \vec{0} \implies a_k = 0$ for all $k$, while the span of $S$ is the set of all vectors generated from $S$, that is, $\text{Span}(S) = \langle S \rangle = \left\{ \sum_k a_k \vec{u_k} : a_k \in F, \vec{u_k} \in S \right\}$. When $\V = \langle S \rangle$, we say $S$ spans $\V$. When a set $\beta$ is both linear independent and spans $\V$, we say $\beta$ a basis of $\V$.

Interestingly, all the bases of $\V$ has the same cardinality! The replacement theorem states that for finite $S$ which spans $\V$ and linear independent $L$, then $|S| \ge |L|$ and we can pick a subset $H$ of $S$ of size $|S| - |L|$ such that $H \cup L$ spans $\V$, which can be proven with induction. Since bases are both linear independent and spans $\V$, all their cardinality are same, and we may define the dimension of vector space $\dim V$ as the size of any basis.

With bases, we may deduce formulae for dimensions. For example, $\dim \U_1 \oplus \U_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus \U_m = \sum_{i} \dim \U_i$ and $\dim \W_1 + \W_2 = \dim \W_1 + \dim \W_2 - \dim \W_1 \cap \W_2$. If we define the quotient space $\V / \W = \{ v + \W : v \in \V \}$ where $v + \W = \{ v + w : w \in \W \}$ are cosets, then $\dim \V/\W = \dim \V - \dim \W$ for finite-dim case. Also define the direct product space $\V_1 \times \V_2 = \{ (x, y) : x \in \V_1, y \in \V_2 \}$, then $\dim \V_1 \times \V_2 = \dim \V_1 + \dim \V_2$.

Why must basis of a vector space exists? For finite-dimensional vector space this is apparent, but not so for infinite-dimensional ones. In fact, the existence of basis is guaranteed by Zorn's lemma, which states that if every chain has upper bound, then there exists a maximal element. Thus, we may simply consider the collection of all linear independent sets, then apply Zorn's lemma on it to show existence.

Linear Transformation

A linear transformation is a linear map between vector spaces. Suppose $\T: \V \rightarrow \W$. What does it means for $\T$ to be "linear"? Intuitively, we have $\T(x + y) = \T(x) + \T(y)$ and $\T(a \cdot x) = a \cdot \T(x)$ for $x, y \in \V$ and $a \in F$. The set of all linear transformations from $\V$ to $\W$ is denoted as $\mathcal{L}(\V, \W)$. Example includes $\T_0$ (zero transformation), $I_\V$ (identity transformation), $L_A : F^n \rightarrow F^m$ (left matrix multiplication).

Range and Null Space

Given a linear transform $\T \in \mathcal{L}(\V, \W)$, we can define the range (image) $\mathcal{R}(\T) := \{ \T(x) : x \in \V \}$ and null space (kernel) $\mathcal{N}(\T) := \{ x \in \V : \T(x) = 0_\W \}$. Consequently, $\T$ is 1-1 (injective) iff $\mathcal{N}(\T) = \{ 0 \}$, onto (surjective) iff $\mathcal{R}(\T) = \W$. A fact to note is that $T$ is uniquely determined by its value at basis of $\V$.

Clearly both the range and the null space are subspaces (of $\W$ and $\V$) from definition. Therefore, we call $\dim \mathcal{R}(\T)$ the rank and $\dim \mathcal{N}(\T)$ as the nullity of $\T$. With manipulation on bases, we may show that $\dim \mathcal{N}(\T) + \dim \mathcal{R}(\T) = \dim \V$, which is the rank-nullity theorem.

Matrix Representation

Similar to the case of vector space, we want to represent a linear transformation by something tangible - matrices. How can we do that along with the concept of bases?

Suppose $\V$ is a finite-dim vector space with ordered basis $\beta = \{ \vec{u_1}, \dots, \vec{u_n} \}$. Then for each $\vec{x} \in \V$, we can represent it as coordinate vector with respect to $\beta$, which is $[\vec{x}]_\beta = (a_1 \; a_2 \; \cdots \; a_n)^T \in F^n$ where $\vec{x} = a_1 \vec{u_1} + \cdots + a_n \vec{u_n}$. The choice to represent $x$ as column vectors is simply out of taste.

If finite-dim $\W$ has ordered basis $\gamma = \{ \vec{w_1}, \cdots, \vec{w_m} \}$, then we can represent $\T(\vec{v_j})$ as $\sum_{i = 1}^{m} a_{ij} \vec{w_i}$. We may define $A = (a_{ij}) = [\T]_\beta^\gamma$ as the matrix representation of $\T$ with respect to $\beta$ and $\gamma$, which visually it just encode the linear transformation in the form $\left(\;[T(\vec{v_1})]_\gamma \; | \cdots | \; [T(\vec{v_n})]_\gamma \; \right)$.

Intuitively, given linear map $\U: \W \rightarrow \Z$, $\U \T : \V \rightarrow \Z$ is linear as well with $[\U \T]_\alpha^\gamma = [\U]_\beta^{\gamma} [\T]_\alpha^{\beta}$, while $[\T(\vec{u})]_\gamma = [\T]_\beta^{\gamma}[\vec{u}]_\beta$, which can be proven with a bunch of summations.

Invertibility

When is a linear transformation $\T: \V \rightarrow \W$ invertible? By definition, there should exists another $\T^{-1}: \W \rightarrow \V$ such that $\T^{-1} \circ \T = I_\V$ and $\T \circ \T^{-1} = I_\W$. In the function sense, we require $\T$ to be bijective, which is true whenever $\dim \V = \text{rank} \; \T = \dim \W$. Naturally, we have $[\T^{-1}]_\gamma^\beta = \left([\T]_\beta^\gamma\right)^{-1}$. We also say $\T$ is an isomorphism between the vector spaces $\V$ and $\W$ and writes $\V \cong \W$. In the finite-dim case, if $\dim \V = n$ we have $\V \cong F^n$, simply by considering the standard representation $\phi_\beta : V \xrightarrow{\sim} F^n$ with $\vec{x} \mapsto [\vec{x}]_\beta$. Similarly, we can consider the space of linear transformation $\mathcal{L}(\V, \W)$ and have $\mathcal{L}(\V, \W) \xrightarrow{\sim} M_{m \times n}(F)$ naturally by $\T \mapsto [\T]_\beta^\gamma$, which $\xrightarrow{\sim}$ refers to isomorphism.

Dual Space

We call a linear map with the form $\V \rightarrow \V$ a linear operator and with the form $\V \rightarrow F$ a linear functional. Alternatively, we can denote $\V^* = \mathcal{L}(\V, F)$ as the dual space, the collection of all linear functionals $f : \V \rightarrow F$. Obviously we have $\dim \V = \dim \V^*$, while $l: \V \rightarrow \V^{**}$ with $l(\vec{v})(f) = f(\vec{v})$ is an isomorphism between $\V$ and $\V^{**}$ whenever $\V$ is finite-dim.

We can define dual map for linear transformation $\T: \V \rightarrow \W$ as well. Define $\T^* : \W^* \rightarrow \V^*$ with $g \mapsto g \circ \T$, then $[\T^*]_{\gamma^*}^{\beta^*} = \left( [\T]_\beta^\gamma \right)^T$, the transpose matrix of $[\T]_\beta^\gamma$. Intuitively, $v_j \xrightarrow{\T} \sum_{i} a_{ij} \cdot w_i \xrightarrow{w_i^*} a_{ij}$, so $\T^*(w_i^*)(v_j) = a_{ij}$.

Change of Coordinates

Let's say the same vector space $\V$ has two bases $\beta$ and $\beta'$. To convert between these two bases, we can obviously define the change of coordinate matrix $Q = [I_\V]_{\beta'}^\beta$, essentially changing coordinates from $\beta'$ to $\beta$. From definition, we have $[v]_{\beta} = Q[v]_{\beta'}$ for all $v \in \V$ and $[\T]_{\beta} = Q^{-1} [T]_{\beta'} Q$.

Specially, if $\beta'$ is the standard basis, then $[L_A]_\gamma = Q^{-1} A Q$. We call two matrices $A \sim B$ similar if they represent the same linear transformation under different bases, that is, when $B = Q^{-1} A Q$ for some invertible $Q$.

Diagonalisation

Sometimes we want to diagonalise a linear operator. Intuitively, when $\T(v) = \lambda v$ where $\T: \V \rightarrow \V$ and $v \in \V$, then we can compute $\T^n(v) = \lambda^n v$ pretty efficiently. When this happens, $v$ is called an eigenvector (whenever $v \ne 0$) and $\lambda$ is the corresponding eigenvalue (eigen- means self-). What we want to do is to find an eigenbasis $\beta$ of $\V$, that is, a basis consists of eigenvectors of $\T$, so that $[\T]_\beta = D$ where $D$ is a diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n)$.

How to find eigenvalues (and eigenvectors)? For $\T(v) = \lambda v$, it must be the case where $[\T]_\beta [v]_\beta = \lambda [v]_\beta$, which means $([\T]_\beta - \lambda I_n) [v]_\beta = 0$ or $\det ([\T]_\beta - \lambda I_n) = 0$. Note that the choice of the basis does not matter at all, since $\det(Q^{-1}AQ - \lambda I_n) = \det Q^{-1} \cdot \det (A - \lambda I_n) \cdot \det Q = \det (A - \lambda I_n)$! Thus we can define the characteristic polynomial of $\T$ as $f_\T(t) = \det([\T]_\beta - tI_n) \in P_n(F)$, and the roots of $f_\T(t)$ are the eigenvalues of $\T$. We may define the eigenspace $\mathcal{E}_\lambda := \mathcal{N}(\T - \lambda I_\V) \subseteq \V$, containing eigenvectors for $\lambda$. Note that eigenvectors in different eigenspaces are linear independent, which can be proven by applying $\T$ on the linear combination and carry out some induction.

For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, we may define the algebraic multiplicity $\mu_\T(\lambda)$ as the order of $\lambda$ as root of $f_\T(t)$, and the geometric multiplicity $\gamma_\T(\lambda) = \dim \mathcal{E}_\lambda \ge 1$. Thus, when $f_\T(t)$ splits over $F$ (can be written as product of linear polynomials), and that $\mu(\lambda) = \gamma(\lambda)$ for all $\lambda$, then $\T$ is diagonalisable. We can in fact shows that $\mu(\lambda) \ge \gamma(\lambda)$, algebraic multiplicity is always greater or equal to geometric multiplicity, with the use of T-cyclic subspaces.

$\T$-cyclic Subspace

Define $\T$-invariant subspace as a subspace $\W \subseteq \V$ such that $\T(\W) \subseteq \W$, which is exactly when we can restrict $\T$ to $\W$ as $\T|_\W$. For any $v \in \V$, we can define $\W = \langle \T^k v : k \in \mathbb{N} \rangle$ as the $\T$-cyclic subspace of $v \in \V$, which is clearly $\T$-invariant as well. Now note that for $\dim \W = k$, we must have $\beta = \{ v, \T v, \dots, \T^{k - 1} v \}$ being the basis of $\W$, which thus we have $f_{\T|_\W}(t) = \det ([\T]_\beta - tI) = (-1)^k (a_0 + a_1 t + \cdots + t^k)$ with $a_0 + a_1 \T + \cdots + \T^k = \O$.

For any $\T$-invariant subspace $\W$, note that $f_{\T|_\W} (t) \mid f_{\T}(t)$ by extending basis of $\W$ to $\V$. Therefore, we can prove the Cayley-Hamilton Theorem that $f_\T(\T) = \O$. Note that the eigenspace $\mathcal{E}_\lambda$ is $\T$-invariant as well. By picking any basis $\gamma$, then $[\T|_{\mathcal{E}_\lambda}]_\gamma = \lambda I$, and so $f_{\mathcal{E}_\lambda}(t) = (\lambda - t)^{\gamma(\lambda)}$. Since $f_{\mathcal{E}_\lambda}(t) \mid f_\V (t)$, we have $\gamma(\lambda) \le \mu(\lambda)$.

Tensor Product Space

A tensor product space is a space which it behaves like multiplying two polynomials. Consider a bilinear transformation $\mu: \V_1 \times \V_2 \rightarrow Y$, then we write $Y = \V_1 \otimes \V_2$ whenever $\mu(\beta_1 \times \beta_2)$ forms a basis for $Y$. For example, when $\V = P(F)$, then $\V \otimes \V = P(F^2)$. We can also treat $\V^* \otimes \W$ as $\mathcal{L}(\V, \W)$ by $(f_i \otimes w_j)(v) = f_i(v) \cdot w_j$ for $f_i$ and $w_j$ in basis of $\V^*$ and $\W$ respectively, and henceforth define trace $\text{Tr}: \V^* \otimes \V \rightarrow F$ by $\text{Tr}(f_i \otimes v_j) = f_i(v_j)$ where $\text{Tr}(\T) = A_{11} + A_{22} + \cdots + A_{nn}$ where $A = [\T]_\beta$, $\beta = \{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ the basis of $\V$. Note that trace is independent of the basis considered, since it is actually "encoded" in the characteristic polynomial.

Inner Product Space

Vector space alone is too abstract to encode some geometric informations. For example, what is the angle between two angles? What is the projection of a vector onto another? What is the distance between two points? All these questions cannot be answered if we do not have some function that does the measurement. Therefore, we accompany the vector space with inner product to handle these geometric questions.

We define the inner product $\langle v, w \rangle$ between two vectors $v, w \in \V$ as any conjugate-symmetric, linear in first argument, and positive-definiteness map. That is, $\langle v, w \rangle = \overline{\langle w, v \rangle}$, $\langle au + v, w \rangle = a \langle u, w \rangle + \langle v, w \rangle$ and $\langle x, x \rangle \gt 0$ for all $x \ne 0$.
