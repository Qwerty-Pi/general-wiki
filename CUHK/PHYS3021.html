Electromagnetic Waves

In a region without electric charges or currents, the electric or magnetic field ($E, B : \R^3 \times \R \to \C$) satisfies the Maxwell equation where \( \Psi = E \) or \( B \) as \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \Psi = 0 \] Assuming \( F \) is separable, i.e., we can write \( \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) \times f(t) \), then \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial_t^2 f}{f} = \frac{\nabla^2 \psi}{\psi} \] with each sides being constants. Naturally, we have \( f(t) = \exp i (\omega t) \) and \( \psi(r) = \exp i (k \cdot r) \) for some constants \( \omega \) and \( k \). Combine together, we have the wave equation \[ \Psi(r, t) = A \exp i (k \cdot r - \omega t)\]

In words, \( \Psi \) is a sinusodial propagating wave with wave vector \( k \), the change in phase due to position, or the propagation direction of the wave; and angular frequency \( \omega \), the change in phase due to time. For EM waves, the dispersion relation is \( \omega = c \cdot |k| \), since EM waves travel at light speed \( c \). In practice, we assume that wave of any (valid) shape can be written as a superposition (sum) of multiple seperable plane waves, which kinda solves the general case.

We recall other concepts in waves. The frequency is \( f = \frac{\omega}{2 \pi} \), the number of complete phase revolutions per unit time. The wavelength is defined as \( \lambda = \frac{2 \pi}{|k|} \), the length of a complete phase. For EM waves, we have \( c = f \cdot \lambda \). Finally, the wavenumber (more in chemsitry) is defined as \( \tilde \nu = \frac{1}{\lambda} \), the reciprocal of wavelength.

Blackbody Radiation and Quantised Energy

Suppose we have a physical object, which can absorbs and emits radiations. We idealise a blackbody as a perfect absorber that absorbs all incoming light (EM waves) and reflect nothing from its surface, then the radiant energy we measured from the blackbody only come from itself. Turns out that for a blackbody, the radiant energy desnity over wavelengths only depends on the temperature.

Classically, we would expect an infrared catastrophe, which the energy density grows uncontrollably as wavelength decreases, which doesn't match the experimental results. Max Planck proposes the idea which we should quantise the energy \( E = nhf \) where \( n \in \N \) and \( h \) is the Planck's constant, which derives the more-accurate Planck's formula for radiant energy density for blackbody as \[ u(f) \, df = 2 \times \frac{4 \pi f^2 \, df}{c^3} \times \frac{hf}{e^{hf/(k_B T)} - 1}\] where the first two terms correspond to the number of modes with frequency within \( [f, f + df] \), and the last term correspond to the average energy per mode.

Photon and Matter Waves

Einstein proposes that light exists in photons, as particles. For a photon of frequency \( f \) and wavevector \( k \), it carries the quantised energy \( E = hf = \hbar \omega \) and momentum \( p = \hbar k \). Compton use X-ray to scatter electrons, proving that light does have momentum. Boltzmann said that the probability of state with energy \( E_n = nhf \) is \( p_n \propto \exp -\frac{E_n}{k_B T} \) and we trust him, so the average number of photons with frequency \( f \) is, namely the Bose-Einstein statistics, \[ \langle n_f \rangle = \frac{1}{e^{hf/(k_B T)} - 1}\]

Afterwards, de Brogile proposes that particles exists as matter waves. For a particle of (relativistic) energy \( E \) and momentum \( p \), its matter wave has \( f = E / h \) and \( \lambda = h / p \). The phase velocity \( v_{ph} = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{c^2}{v} \), where the group velocity \( v_{g} = \partial_k \omega = v \). It is really the group velocity that carries information.

Schrodinger's Equation

Classically, in Newtonian mechanics we have \( F = ma \), where the force governs the movement of a particle. In a similar manner, the Schrodinger's Equation governs the evolution of a quantum wave as \[ i \hbar \partial_t \Psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \Psi + V \Psi \] where \( V : \R^3 \to \R \) is the potential and \( \Psi : \R^3 \times \R \to \C \) is the quantum wave. Effectively, the evolution of \( \Psi \) depends on a "kinetic" component \( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \) and a "potential" component \( V \).

Schrodinger initially interprets \( \Psi \) as the charge density (in e.g. hydrogen atom), but it rather should be interpreted as probability density, which the density is \( |\Psi^2| \) or \( \Psi^* \Psi \). As a PDF, the amplitude of quantum waves doesn't matter (unlike EM waves), and we expect \( \Psi \) to be normalised where \( \int |\Psi|^2 dV = 1 \). Formally, this means \( \Psi \) is a \( L^2 \) (square-integrable) function.

Time-Independent Schrodinger

Since Schodinger is a linear PDE, its solution forms a vector space. If we assume \( \Psi \) is seperable where \( \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) \times f(t) \), then \[ i \hbar \frac{\partial_t f}{f} = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\nabla^2 \psi}{\psi} + V \] since LHS only depends on time and RHS only depends on position, both sides equal to a constant(!) say \( E \) (energy), and \[ \partial_t f = \frac{E}{\hbar i} f \qquad\qquad \left( - \frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2m} + V \right) \psi = E \psi \] The first equation simply implies \( f = \exp (-i\omega t) \), while the second equation is more interesting. By defining the Hamiltonian operator \[ \hat H = -\dfrac{\hbar^2 \nabla^2}{2m} + V \] we have \[ \hat H \psi = E \psi \] Analogous to the notation in linear algebra, we call \( \psi \) an eigenstate or an eigenfunction, where \( E \) is the corresponding eigenvalue.

\( L^2 \) Inner Product Space and Fourier Transform

Consider the \( L^2 \) inner product space with inner product defined as \( \ip{\psi_1}{\psi_2} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi_1^* \psi_2 \, dx \). Then quantum wave \( \psi \) is normalised iff \( \ip{\psi}{\psi} = 1 \), or \( \psi \) is with unit norm. A natural question is: how can plane waves \( e^{ikx} \) be \( L^2 \), when its integration clearly tends to infinity? In reality, we implicitly consider the plane waves to have infinitesimal decay, that is \( \lim_{\eta \to 0} e^{ikx - \eta |x|} \), making it normalisable.

Now, we want to write quantum wave \( \psi \) into the superposition of plane waves, namely Fourier Transform as \[ \psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int \tilde \psi (k) e^{ikx} \, dk \] Turns out its inverse is very simple: simply take \[ \tilde \psi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \int \psi(x) e^{-ikx} \, dx \]

Define the Dirac delta \( \delta \) be an infinitely sharp smooth function where \( \delta(x) = \infty \) when \( x = 0 \), \( \delta(x) = 0 \) when \( x \ne 0 \) and \( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x) \, dx = 1 \). Then, \( \int e^{ikx} \, dx = \int e^{ikx - \eta |x|} \, dx = 2 \pi \delta(k) \), which means \( \ip{e^{iq}}{e^{ik}} = 2 \pi \delta(k - q) \). With \( \delta \), we can show that the above inverse relation indeed holds.
