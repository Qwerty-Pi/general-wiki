Statistics and Data Analysis

In probability, we compute how likely stuff will happen, or the expectation for numeric results base on known probabilistic models. For instance, you are given a fair coin, a fair dice, or the fact that the bus comes every twenty minutes. However, in real life scenarios, we might not always exactly know the probability model - what if the dice is somehow unfair, or you don't actually know the time gap between two buses? Statistics is the art of recovering the probabilistic model with some confidence via the analysis of data.

In the first section, our focus is to estimate the parameters given data generated by a distribution. In the second section, our focus is to analysis higher dimensional data.

Parameter Estimation

Suppose we are given a sequence of random variables \( X = ( X_1, \dots, X_n ) \) while we observe the values \( x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \). We call \( x \) the observations. For which \( X = (X_1, \dots, X_n) \) are independent and identically distributed (iid), we call this a random sample of size \( n \).

Summary Statistics

Suppose \( X \) is a random sample of size \( n \). What useful information can we extract from \( X \) without any extra assumptions? We call a function that only depends on the values of \( X \) a statistic, and so what we interest in are the summary statistics.

Recall that we define the expected value \( \E[X] = \mu \) and the variance \( \Var(X) = \E[(X - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2 \) as the summary of the distribution. How about the sample mean and sample variance? We define sample mean as \[ \overline{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} X_i \] and sample variance as \[ S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} (X_i - \overline{X})^2 \] The sample mean is defined fairly intuitively, while note the \( n - 1 \) for sample variance is due to the fact that we want \( \E[S^2] = \sigma^2 \) - note that \( \mu \) and \( \overline{X} \) is not identical.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Given a random sample \( X \). Naturally, we can use either PMF (discrete) or PDF (continuous) to compute the likelihood for \( X = x \) to happen: since the random variables are all independent, we can simply multiply their probability mass or density together. Formally, we write \( f(x; \theta) = \prod_i f(x_i; \theta) \) as the joint PMF / PDF of \( X \), where the ; is to emphasize the fact that \( f(x ; \theta) \) depends on the unknown parameter \( \theta \), which \( \theta \) can be a vector as well as scalar.

The Maximum Likelihood Estimation is based on a simple idea: Find the parameter \( \theta \) that maximise the likelihood \( f(x; \theta) \) given the observations \( x \). We denote \( L(\theta) = f(x; \theta) \) as the likelihood of \( \theta \), while the log-likelihood of \( \theta \) is defined as \( \ell (\theta) = \log L(\theta) \). Hence, the Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) \( \hat \theta (x) \) is the value of \( \theta \) that maximises \( L(\theta) \), or equivalently \( \ell(\theta) \) for given \( x \).

We can write \( \hat \theta (X) \) as a function on the random variables in \( X \) as well, which is then called the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE). Specifically, the estimator must not depend on the parameter \( \theta \) because it is unknown, that is, the estimator should be a statistic. Note that estimate is a computed value whereas estimator is itself a random variable.

Estimator Bias

Given an estimator \( \hat \theta \) of \( \theta \), how may us comment on how good it can estimate? A simple definition is the bias \[ b(\hat \theta) = \E[\hat \theta - \theta] = \E[\hat \theta] - \theta \] When \( b(\hat \theta) = 0 \) we say \( \hat \theta \) is unbiased for \( \theta \). Similarly, we can define the mean squared error (MSE) as \[ \text{MSE}(\hat \theta) = \E[(\hat \theta - \theta)^2] \] Let \( \mu = \E[\hat \theta] \), then \( \text{MSE}(\hat \theta) = \E[(\hat \theta - \mu) - (\theta - \mu)]^2 = \Var(\hat \theta) + [b(\hat \theta)]^2 \). That is, for a good estimator \( \hat \theta \), we want both the variance and the bias to be small. As a similar concept, we also define the standard error (SE) as \[ \text{SE}(\hat \theta) = \sqrt{\Var(\hat \theta)} \]

Note that MLE does not imply unbiased. For instance, consider \( X_i \sim \Norm(\mu, \sigma) \) or \[ L(\mu, \sigma) = \prod_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp \frac{-(x_i - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\] Then \[ \ell(\mu, \sigma) = -\frac{n}{2} \log 2 \pi - n \log \sigma - \frac{\sum (x_i - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2} \] Hence \[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \frac{-2(\sum x_i - \mu)}{2 \sigma^2}\] and \[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma} = - \frac{n}{\sigma} + \frac{\sum (x_i - \mu)^2}{\sigma^3}\] By solving \( \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma} = 0 \), we know by multivariable calculus that the MLEs are \( \hat \mu = \overline{X} \) and \( \hat \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum (X_i - \overline{X})^2 \). However, \( \E[\hat \sigma^2] = \frac{n - 1}{n} \sigma^2 \) which means \( \hat \sigma^2 \) is biased.

Confidence Intervals

Suppose we are happily given a standard normal \( Z \sim \Norm(0, 1) \). We can give confidence intervals \( [a, b] \) as in how likely the value will land in that interval. As usual, we take \( \Phi \) as the CDF of \( Z \), and let \( z_\alpha \) be a constant where \( \Phi(z_\alpha) = 1 - \alpha \). Then we say \( 100 (1 - \alpha) \% \) Confidence Interval (CI) as \( (-z_{\alpha/2}, z_{\alpha/2}) \), and the \( 100 (1 - \alpha) \% \) Upper (Lower) Confidence Limit as \( z_{\alpha} \)(\( -z_{\alpha}\)).

How can we compute CI for estimations? Suppose \( X \) is a random sample of \( \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2) \), then \( \overline{X} \sim \Norm\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \), and so \[ \frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \sim \Norm(0, 1) \] With this, we can say the \( 100 (1 - \alpha) \% \) central CI for \( \mu \) is \[ \left(\overline{X} \pm z_{\alpha / 2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \] By invoking the beloved Central Limit Theorem (CLT), we can generalise this to arbitrary distributions.

Linear Regression

Most of the time, we will deal with relationships between different variables. The simplest case is the linear regression where \( Y_i = \alpha + \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i \) for all \( i \) with \( \varepsilon_i \sim \Norm(0, \sigma^2) \) as the errors. How can we estimate \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \)? Once again, with MLE. Suppose \( \sigma^2 \) is known, then \( Y_i \sim \Norm(\alpha + \beta x_i, \sigma^2) \) and \[ \ell(\alpha, \beta) = -\frac{n}{2} \log(2 \pi \sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)^2 \] Hence the MLE is also minimising the sum of squares \( S(\alpha, \beta) = \sum (y_i - \alpha - \beta x_i)^2 \), and so MLEs of \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \) are also called the least square estimators. By differentiating stuff, we have \[ \hat \alpha = \frac{\sum x_i^2 \sum y_i - \sum x_i \sum x_i y_i} {n \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2}\] \[ \hat \beta = \frac{n \sum x_i y_i - \sum x_i \sum y_i}{n \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2}\] We say the fitted regression line as \( y = \hat \alpha + \hat \beta x \). Naturally, we want to know the bias and the variances. By offsetting \( w_i = x_i - \overline{x} \), we have \[ \hat \beta = \frac{\sum w_i y_i}{\sum w_i^2} \] \[ \hat \alpha = \overline{y} - \hat \beta \overline{x} \] We may show \( \E[\hat \beta] = \beta \) and so \( \E[\hat \alpha] = \alpha \), which means both \( \hat \alpha \) and \( \hat \beta \) are unbiased. We also see that \( \Var(\hat \beta) \) is a weighted sum of \( \Var(\varepsilon_i) \), namely \[ \Var(\hat \beta) = \frac{\sum w_i^2 \Var(\varepsilon_i)}{\sum w_i^2}\]We can somewhat trivially generalise this by adding more variables, say \( Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 z_i + \varepsilon_i \), whereas the ideas for MLE or least square would be more or less equivalent.

Fit of Model

We wish to assess how "good" our linear regression is. Is our parameters accurate? Or even is linear regression a good choice for the problem?

We call the fitted value \( \hat y_i = \hat \alpha + \hat \beta x_i \) for \( 1 \le i \le n \) and the residue as \( e_i = y_i - \hat y_i \). The residue sum of squares (RSS) is defined as \( \text{RSS} = \sum e_i^2 \), whereas the residual standard error (RSE) is defined as \( \text{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n - 2}\text{RSS}} \), noting \( \E[\sum e_i^2] = (n - 2) \cdot \sigma^2 \).

First, if our linear model is correct, the residue \( e_i \) plot against \( \hat y_i \) should show no pattern. A certain pattern indicates that maybe we should consider another model. Second, we have assumed that \( \varepsilon_i \) has same variance for all \( i \) - if this does not happen (heteroscedasticity), then we either need to consider \( \log Y \) or \( \sqrt{Y} \) to reduce heteroscedasticity, or use weighted least square instead by weighing observations with small variance more.

Third, how can we rule out outliers? We can define the standardised residuals as \( r_i = \frac{e_i}{\text{SE}(e_i)} \), and list those with \( |r_i| \gt 3 \) as possible outliers. After some toxic computations, we can show \( \Var(e_i) = \sigma^2 (1 - h_i) \), where \[ h_i = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{w_i^2}{\sum w_i^2} \] is the leverage for the \( i \)-th observation. Since \( \E[e_i] = 0 \), we have \( \Var(e_i) = \E[e_i^2] \), which also shows why RSE is defined like this.

Finally, high leverage points. Since \( \Var(e_i) = \sigma^2 (1 - h_i) \), large \( h_i \) make \( \Var(e_i) \) small, which pulls the regression line closer to that point. A rule of thumb is to treat all points double the average leverage \( \overline h = \frac{2}{n} \) as high leverage points.

Data Analysis in Higher Dimensions

Now, we move on to clustering of data. Given a bunch of data, how can we derive a way to group the data in a make-sense way? For instance, by different lengths measured from crabs, perhaps we can already tell the gender and the colour of the crabs.

Suppose there are \( p \) different variables, and for each observation, we record the values for each of the variables. Then, we introduce the data matrix as \( X \in M_{n \times p} \), where the rows are observations and the columns are variables, with \( X_{ij} \) being the observed value of the \( j \)-th variable for the \( i \)-th observation.

Multivariate Normal Distribution

We can generalise normal distribution to multivariate normal distribution (MVN). We say \( X \sim \Norm_p(\mu, \Sigma) \) for mean \( \mu \in \R^p \) and covariance matrix \( \Sigma \in \R_{p \times p} \) (which is symmetric and positive-definite) if the PDF of \( X \) is \[ f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \right)\] Which the form is strikingly similar to that of single variable. Specially, if we write \( X = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p)^T \) as random variables, then \( \Cov[X] = \Sigma \). Since I like gf, we should prove this with moment generating function instead of linear algebra. We have the magical formula (we should really use this as the definition instead) that for \( X \sim \Norm(\mu, \Sigma) \), \[ M_X(t) = \exp\left[t^T \mu + \frac{1}{2} t^T \Sigma t\right] \] and so \( M_{AX + b}(t) = \E[\exp(t^T (AX + b))] = \E[\exp (t^T b)] \cdot M_X(A^Tt) = \exp\left[t^T(A\mu + b) + \frac{1}{2} t^T A \Sigma A^T t\right]\), which means \[ AX + b \sim \Norm(A\mu + b, A \Sigma A^T) \] Let \( Z \sim \Norm(0, I)\) be the standard normal. By writing \( \Sigma = A A^T \), then \( X = AZ + \mu \sim \Norm(\mu, \Sigma) \), and clearly \( \Cov[X] = \Cov[AZ] = A \Cov[Z] A^T = \Sigma \).

Now, clearly we have variance \( \Var(X_i) = \Sigma_{ii} \), covariance \( \Cov(X_i, X_j) = \Sigma_{ij} \) and correlation \( \rho(X_i, X_j) = \frac{\Sigma_{ij}}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{ii} \Sigma_{jj}}} \). To find the MLE for this, we need some linear algebra magic: Note that up to constant, we have \[ \ell(\mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{2} \log |\Sigma^{-1}| - \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{1}{2} (x_i - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x_i - \mu) \] Hence we have the MLEs \( \hat \mu = \frac{1}{n} \sum x_i \) and \( \hat \Sigma = \frac{1}{n} \sum (x_i - \hat \mu)^T (x_i - \hat \mu) \). Since \( \E[\hat \Sigma] = \frac{n}{n - 1} \Sigma \), we call the sample covariance matrix as \[ S = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} (x_i - \hat \mu)^T (x_i - \hat \mu) \] If \( X \) is mean-centred, then it nicely becomes \( S = \frac{1}{n - 1} X^T X \). Similarly, we define the sample correlation matrix \( R = W^{-1/2} S W^{-1/2} \), where \( W = \text{diag}(S_{11}, S_{22}, \dots, S_{pp}) \).

Principal Component Analysis

What is Principal Component Analysis (PCA)? Effectively, we are given data matrix \( X \) as points in \( \R^p \). It is very hard to visualise them, so it is useful to project them to some lines or planes. However, what lines or planes to pick? One of the direction is that we want to maximise the variance, since variance is what distinguish the points from one and other. What we try to do is to read the data under another orthonormal basis instead of just the boring standard basis.

Consider the sample covariance matrix \( S \) of \( X \). Since \( S \) is positive-definite and symmetric, there exists an orthonormal eigenbasis \( \beta = \{ \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_p \} \) where \( S \alpha_i = \lambda_i \alpha_i \) and \( \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_p \gt 0 \). Clearly, we should be picking exactly these directions to project since the variance in \( \alpha \) is \( \alpha^T S \alpha = \alpha^T \sum_i \lambda_i \langle \alpha, \alpha_i \rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i \langle \alpha, \alpha_i \rangle^2 \). We call \( V = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_p) \) the loadings matrix, which we can convert to the scores matrix via \( Z = XV \), which is the values in the principal components. If the variance in one particular variable of \( X \) is particularly large, then clearly our \( V \) will be skewed, so we may use instead the sample correlation matrix \( R \) for work instead. Also note that PCA also provides us approximations to \( X \) as \( \tilde X = z_1 w_1^T \).

Clustering Methods

Now, let's introduce some clustering methods - Given a bunch of points in \( \R^p \), how can we group them in make-sense ways?

The first method is the K-mean Clustering. Let's say we want to partition the points in a way that minimise the sum of distance inside clusters, that is, \( [n] = C_1 \uplus C_2 \uplus \cdots \uplus C_K \), where we want to minimise \[ \sum_{k = 1}^{K} \frac{1}{|C_k|} \sum_{i, j \in C_k} \Vert x_i - x_j \Vert^2_2 = \sum_{k = 1}^{K} 2 \sum_{i \in C_k} \Vert x_i - \mu_k \Vert^2_2 \] Considering the Euclidean norm. Then the heuristic is simple: Randomly assign points to groups, compute the mean within group, assign each point to nearest mean, repeat indefinitely. This is called the K-mean Clustering.

The second method is the Hierarchical Clustering. By introducing metric between groups, we can merge the "nearest" groups repeatedly to have a hierarchy. We can have different metric modes, including Single Linkage (take min), Complete Linkage (take max) and Group Average (take average). A dendrogram can then be drawn (essentially a tree).
