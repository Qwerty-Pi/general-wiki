MATH3280 Introductory Probability

Probability is the art of dealing with uncertainty. We wish to handle probability gracefully using an axiomatic approach, so everything can be well-defined and computed at ease.

Probability Space

Consider any experiment where the outcome is uncertain. The sample space \( S \) is defined as the set of all possible outcomes, where any subset \( E \subset S \) is called as an event. With events, we may introduce notations such as \( E \cup F \) (union), \( E \cap F \) or equivalently \( EF \) (intersection), \( \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( \bigcap_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( E \subset F \) (subset) and \( E^c \) (complement), which essentially just set notations.

Axioms of Probability

How can we define probability for events? One of the ways is perhaps just say we define \( n(E) \) be the number of times the event \( E \) occurs within \( n \) experiments, then take \( P(E) := \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n(E)}{n} \). However, this is not elementary enough, since we haven't prove the convergence yet! It would be obviously better if we may resort to simpler axioms.

Axiomatically, probability is defined as a function $P$ that maps from event space to \( \mathbb{R} \), where

  1. For every event \( E \), \( 0 \le P(E) \le 1 \),
  2. \( P(S) = 1 \) for sample space \( S \),
  3. For countable (possibly infinite) collection \( \{ E_i \} \) of mutually exclusive events, we have \( P(\bigcup E_i) = \sum P(E_i)\).

With these alone, we may deduce interesting stuff like the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle, which states that \[ P\left(\bigcup E_i\right) = \sum_{S \subset [n]} (-1)^{|S|} \bigcap_{j \in S} E_j \] Which can be proven with induction (or fancier stuff).

There are some trivial facts: First, when the number of outcomes is finite and each outcome has the same probability, then the probability of an event can be computed via simply counting outcomes. Second, for monotone sequence of events \( \{ E_i \} \), we have \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(E_n) = P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n\right) \]

Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is introduced when we try to consider stuff like "assume event \( F \) already happened, what is the probability of event \( E \) happens as well". We denote this conditional probability as \( P(E | F) \), and we trivially have \[ P(E | F) = \frac{P(E \cap F)}{P(F)} \] whenever \( P(F) \gt 0 \). We can chain conditional probability together in the form \[ P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) P(B | A) P(C | A \cap B)\]

One of the key concepts revolving conditional probability is the Baye's Formula: Let's say we already know for events \( E \) and \( F \) the probabilities \( P(E) \), \( P(F|E^c) \) and \( P(F|E) \), what would the conditional probability \( P(E | F) \) be? You may imagine \( E \) represents a hypothesis and \( F \) represents an test; then we would want to know what the probability that the hypothesis holds is, given that the test returns positive.

The Baye's Formula or the law of total probability is simply \[ P(F) = \sum_i P(E_i) \cdot P(F|E_i) \] where \( \{ E_i \} \) partitions the sample space \( S \). With this, we can compute \( P(E | F) \) as \[ P(E | F) = \frac{P(F | E)P(E)}{P(F | E)P(E) + P(F | E^c)P(E^c)}\] Which is just simple manipulations.
