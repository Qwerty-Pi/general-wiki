Introduction

Quantum Mechanics

Schrödinger equation states that $$ i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \ket{\psi(t)} = \hat H \ket{\psi(t)} $$

We don't actually care about quantum mechanics, so the only thing important is that this is an unitary action on the state vector.

Linear Algebra

Hilbert Space is a Complete Inner-Product Space. The completeness is in the metric sense.

Dirac Notation is essentially instead of using arrows, we use $\ket{\psi}$ and $\bra{\psi}$ to denote column and row vectors respectively, with $\bra{\psi} = \ket{\psi}^{\dagger}$ and $A^\dagger$ being the conjugate transpose of $A$. This means the inner product of $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$ can be conveniently written as $\langle \phi \ket{\psi}$. Projection Operator $\Pi_{\psi}$ along $|\psi\rangle$ can also be written conveniently as $\ket{\psi} \bra{\psi} $. We also abbreviate $\ket{k} = e_k$ and $\ket{\pm} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{0} \pm \ket{1})$. The basis $\{ \ket{0}, \ket{1} \}$ is called the computational basis, whereas $\{ \ket{+}, \ket{-} \}$ is called the Hadamard basis.

Some unitary matrices that are often used are Pauli Matrices $X$ (bit-flip), $Y$ (combination), $Z$ (phase-flip) and Hadamard Matrix $H$: $$ X = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad Y = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad Z = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The spectral decomposition of Hermitian (observable) $A$ can be stated as $A = \sum_{m} \lambda_m \Pi_m = \sum_{m} \lambda_m \ket{\psi_m} \bra{\psi_m}$.

Consider a bipartite Hilbert Space $\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$. For $\ket{\psi} \in \mathcal{H}$, if $\ket{\psi} = \ket{\psi_A} \otimes \ket{\psi_B} $ for some $\ket{\psi_A} \in \mathcal{H}_A$ and $\ket{\psi_B} \in \mathcal{H}_B$, then $\ket{\psi}$ is called separable, and entangled if otherwise. We can define analogously for unitary operators as well.

The Four Postulates

The four postulates are stated below:

  1. A quantum physical system is described by a state vector, which is a unit vector $ \ket{\psi} $ in some Hilbert Space.
  2. The evolution of closed quantum system is described by a unitary action $U$, that is $ \ket{\psi'} = U \ket{\psi}$.
  3. The measurement of quantum system can be described by a collection of measurement operators $\{ M_m \}$ satisfying the completeness condition $ \sum_{m} M_m^{\dagger} M_m = I $, where the outcome is $m$ and the state vector collapses to $ \widehat{M_m \ket{\psi}} $ with probability $|| M_m \ket{\psi} ||^2$.
  4. The state space of a composite quantum system is the tensor product of the state spaces of the component physical systems.

Basic Quantum-Exclusive Effects

Elitzur-Vaidman Bomb

Consider the set-up where there is maybe a bomb inside a suitcase, and you want to know whether the suitcase is innocent or malicious. We model this as a quantum state $\ket{\psi}$, which is $\ket{0}$ initially.

  • If the suitcase is innocent, then $ \ket{\psi'} = \ket{\psi} $.
  • If the suitcase is malicious, then $\ket{\psi} $ is measured in the computational basis. If the outcome is $0$, nothing happens. If the outcome is $1$, the bomb explodes and game over.

Classically, this is unsolvable at all. However, if we talk in quantum, there is an algorithm to solve this. Take $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\pi}{2T}$ for some large $T \in \mathbb{N}$, then for $i = 0$ to $T - 1$, update $\ket{\psi_i} = R_{\varepsilon} | \psi_{i - 1} \rangle$ and query using $ \ket{\psi_i} $. Finally, measure $ \ket{\psi_T} $ in computational basis. If the outcome is $1$, then report innocent, and report malicious otherwise.

  • If the suitcase is innocent, $ \ket{\psi_T} = R_{T \varepsilon} \ket{0} = \ket{1}$, so innocent is always reported.
  • If the suitcase is malicious, there is a $\sin^2 \varepsilon$ chance in each round that the bomb is triggered. Otherwise, $\ket{\psi_i}$ is reset to $\ket{0}$. So malicious is always reported with explosion probability not exceeding $ \varepsilon^{-1} \sin^2 \varepsilon \le \varepsilon$, by the union bound.

No-Cloning Theorem

Classically, we can always copy a bit deterministically as $b \mapsto (b, b)$. We can also always "re-prepare" known quantum states. However, a universal quantum algorithm for $|\psi \rangle \mapsto (\ket{\psi}, \ket{\psi} ) $ cannot exist.

The proof is simple - Assume on contrary that such unitary operator $U$ exists. Then $U \ket{\psi, e} = \ket{\psi, \psi}$ and $U \ket{\phi, e} = \ket{\phi, \phi}$ for arbitrary $\ket{\psi} \ne \ket{\phi}$. Then $ \ip{\psi, e}{\phi, e} = \ip{\psi, \psi}{\phi, \phi}$, implying $\ip{\psi}{\phi} = \ip{\psi}{\phi}^2$ and that $\ip{\psi}{\phi}$ is either $0$ or $1$ - So $U$ is not universal at all.

Quantum Teleportation

How can we transfer a qubit? We first introduce the CNOT (Controlled-Not) Gate and the EPR Pair. The CNOT gate acts on two qubits as $ \text{CNOT} \ket{a, b} = \ket{a, a \oplus b}$, where the EPR Pair is the entangled state $\ket{\Phi^{+}} := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\ket{00} + \ket{11}) = \text{CNOT}(H \oplus I) \ket{00}$.
