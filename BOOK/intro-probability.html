Introduction to Probability

Probability is the art of computing the likelihood of something that will happens. We want to accurately compute and represent probabilities using a systematic way. As such, we wish to have concrete axiomatic definitions on our probabilistic model.

Sample Space and Probability

Probability Space

A probability space \( (\Omega, \F, \P) \) is a space where \( \Omega \) is the sample space, \( \F \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Omega) \) (power set of \( \Omega \)) is the event space and \( \P: \F \to [0, 1]\) is the probability function. In common language, the sample space \( \Omega \) is the set of all possible results, the event space \( \mathcal{F} \) is a collection of events which is subsets of \( \Omega \), and the probability function \( \P \) assigns a probability to each of the events.

A philosophical question is - why do we not assign probability to the sample space directly? The issue being when \( \Omega \) is uncountable say \( [0, 1] \) and \( \P \) is the uniform distribution, then for any \( x \in [0, 1] \), the probability of getting \( x \) is always zero! It doesn't make sense nor useful, and so we assign probabilities to events instead.

Next, what are the axioms that the probability function \( \P \) must satisfy? Turns out there are only three axioms that it must satisfy:
  1. (Nonnegativity) \( \P(A) \ge 0 \) for all event \( A \in \F \).
  2. (\( \sigma \)-additivity) For sequence of disjoint events \( \{ A_i \}_{i \in I} \subseteq \F \) of cardinality at most countable, we have \[ \sum_{i \in I} \P(A_i) = \P\left(\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i\right)\]
  3. (Normalisation) \( \P(\Omega) = 1 \).

Formally, we require \( \F \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Omega) \) to be a \( \sigma \)-algebra, which means \( \Omega \in \F \) and \( \F \) is closed under complement and countable union, so that if \( A \) is an event, then \( A^c \) is also an event; and if \( A_i \) for \( i \in I \) are events, then \( \cup_{i \in I} A_i \) is also an event whenever \( I \) is at most countable.

In the following, we take these three probability axioms as granted, and make deductions base on them. By assuming these probability axioms alone, we may deduce laws such as for events \( A \) and \( B \), \( A \subseteq B \implies \P(A) \le \P(B) \) and \( \P(A \cup B) = \P(A) + \P(B) - \P(A \cap B) \).

Conditional Probability

Given an event \( B \) occurs, what is the conditional probability that event \( A \) occurs? Clearly if \( \P(B) = 0 \), then the conditional probability is meaningless, so we might as well assume \( \P(B) \gt 0 \). Clearly we have \[ \P(A \sep B) = \frac{\P(A \cap B)}{\P(B)}\] Indeed, we may treat \( \P(\cdot \sep B)\) as a probability function on sample space \( B \) as well.

Clearly, from definition we have the multiplication rule \[ \P\left( \cap_{i = 1}^n A_i \right) = \P(A_1) \cdot \P(A_2 \sep A_1) \cdots \P(A_n \sep \cap_{i = 1}^{n - 1} A_i)\]

Suppose \( \Omega = A_1 \uplus A_2 \uplus \cdots \uplus A_n \) is a partition where \( \P(A_i) \gt 0 \). For an event \( B \), we can write out the Total Probability Theorem \[ \P(B) = \sum_{i = 1}^n \P(A_i) \cdot \P(B \sep A_i) \] And naturally, we have the Bayes' Rule \[ \P(A_k \sep B) = \frac{\P(A_k) \cdot P(B \sep A_k) }{\sum_{i = 1}^n \P(A_i) \cdot \P(B \sep A_i)} \] One of the interpretation for this is that \( A_i \) is actual possibilities (such as positive / negative for a disease) while \( B \) is some observations (such as test for the disease). We can then use the Bayes' Rule to compute the probability of some events happening upon some observations.

Independent Events

A key notion revolving probability is independent events. In layman's term, we say \( A \) and \( B \) are independent when when \( A \) and \( B \) are "irrelevant". How shall we define independence using probabilities?

Using conditional probabilities, we say events \( A \) and \( B \) are independent when \[ \P(A) = \P(A \sep B) \] Which effectively means knowing whether \( B \) happens doesn't help you determine whether \( A \) happens. An equivalent way of saying the condition for independence is \[ \P(A) \cdot \P(B) = \P(A \cap B) \] We may readily generalise this definition to collection of events \( \{ A_i \}_{i \in I} \), which we say \( \{ A_i \} \) is independent iff \[ \P\left( \bigcap_{i \in S} A_i \right) = \prod_{i \in S} \P(A_i) \] For all \( S \) being finite subset of \( I \).

We may also define conditional independence over the probability space with probability function \( \P(\cdot \sep C) \) for event \( C \) with \( \P(C) \gt 0 \), while the definition readily follows.

Discrete Random Variables

Many of the time, we try to assign a numerical value to each of the outcomes, for example, number of pips on dice. We may formally represent them via random variables. A random variable is a real-valued function \( X : \Omega \to \R \), where we define the range \( \Omega_X = X(\Omega) \).

Unfortunately, we need to handle the case where \( \Omega_X \) is at most countably infinite (say \( \N \)) and uncountably infinite (say \( \R \)) separately, since summation over countable sets is (more) well-defined, but we need to use integration for summation for uncountable range of \( X \). We refer the first case as discrete random variable, and the second case as continuous random variable. We will use the same set of notations for both case. However, their actual computation would be slightly different due to above limitations.

In this section, we focus on the case of discrete random variables, that is, when \( \Omega_X \) is at most countably infinite.

Probability Mass Function

To represent a discrete random variable \( X : \Omega \to \R \), we may use the Probability Mass Function (PMF).
