MATH2070 Algebraic Structure

Introduction

Why do we study algebraic structure? Because we want to abstract and generalise theorems whenever possible.

In the following, we pay our focus on groups and rings, provide abstractions of many well-known stuff, for example set of integers, matrices, permutations or functions.

Group

A group is a set $G$ equipped with a group operation $\cdot : G \times G \rightarrow G$, such that it is associative while both identity and inverse exists. Basically we want to have a set of conditions that is minimal so that it can apply to more different objects, but also the conditions should be "strong enough" for us to deduce something useful. We call a group abelian if the group operation is commutative, and the order $|G|$ of a group being the cardinality of $G$.

There are tons of examples, such as $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$, $(\mathbb{Q}^\times, \cdot)$, $(U_m, \cdot)$ (Roots of Unity), $(S_x, \circ)$ (Bijective functions from $X$ to itself), or things like $\text{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (General Linear Group, set of $n \times n$ matrices with determinant non-zero), $\text{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (Special Linear Group, set of $n \times n$ matrices with determinant $1$) or $(\mathbb{Z}_n, +_n)$ (Modular Arithmetic). All these satisfy the condition being a group, and hence all the laws and rules deduced henceforth can be applied on them.

To abstract the concept of functions, we can consider categories as well. A category $\mathcal{C}$ consists of objects $\text{Obj}(\mathcal{C})$ and morphisms between objects $\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ where $A, B \in \text{Obj}(\mathcal{C})$ where identity exists, which is $1_A \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, A)$, and we can compose morphisms together, $(f, g) \mapsto g \circ f$ with $\circ$ associative. Examples includes the categories of set and vector spaces. Specially, we call a morphism $f \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(A, B)$ isomorphism if there exists $g \in \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(B, A)$ such that $g \circ f = 1_A$ and $f \circ g = 1_B$, where automorphism is isomorphism from $A$ to itself. Interestingly (actually from definition), the set of all automorphisms $\text{Aut}_{\mathcal{C}}(A)$ also forms a group!

Common Groups

Cyclic Groups are groups that can be generated by a single element, or $G = \langle g \rangle = \{ g^n : n \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ for some $g \in G$, which $g$ is called a generator of $G$ (which need not be unique). We define similarly the order of $g$, $|g|$, being the cardinality of $\langle g \rangle$. Somewhat obviously, we have $|g| = \infty \implies \langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}$ and $|g| \lt \infty \implies \langle g \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}_{|g|}$, where $G \cong H$ means $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic. Clearly all cylic group are abelian as well.

Symmetric Groups are groups containing permutations, which the elements of $S_X$ are $\sigma: X \rightarrow X$ where $\sigma$ are bijective. Definition tells us that $|S_n| = n!$, while $S_n$ is not abelian for all $n \ge 3$. We can denote cycles as $(i_1 \; i_2 \; \cdots \; i_k )$, which is the permutation mapping $i_j$ to $i_{j + 1}$ for all $1 \le j \le k - 1$ and $i_k$ to $i_1$, fixing all other values. Clearly we can decompose every permutation into disjoint cycles, which then we can consider the number of inversions. Denote $A_n$ the alternating group which contains all permutations that (the number of inversions) is even, we know that $A_n$ is simple for all $n \ge 5$, that is, it does not contain proper normal subgroup.

Dihedral Groups are groups mimicking rotating and reflecting a regular polygon, where the elements are $ D_n = \{ \text{id}, r, r^2, \cdots, r^{n - 1}, s, rs, \cdots, r^{n - 1}s \} $ Which $r$ refers to rotating the polygon by $\frac{2\pi}{n}$ radian, where $s$ refers to reflecting the polygon at a certain direction. Clearly $|D_n| = 2n$, and $D_n = \langle r, s \rangle$, which means $D_n$ is generated by the two generators $r$ and $s$.

Subgroup

Subgroups are groups that are contained in another group, inheriting the same binary operator. We write $H \le G$ meaning that $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. For the sake of closure, we require that for all $g, h \in H$, $g \cdot h, g^{-1} \in H$ as well. The trivial subgroups includes $\{e\} \le G$ and $G \le G$, where $H \lt G$ means $H$ is proper subgroup of $G$ with $H \ne G$. For example, we have $\mathbb{Z} \lt \mathbb{Q} \lt \mathbb{R} \lt \mathbb{C}$ (under $+$), $n \mathbb{Z} = \langle n \rangle \lt \mathbb{Z}$ (cyclic subgroup) for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $D_n \lt S_n$ (act on regular polygon) and $A_n \lt S_n$ (even permutations).

For cyclic subgroups, there are some intuitive facts. For example, $\langle g \rangle = \bigcap_{\{H : g \in H \le G \}} H$, which means the smallest set containing $g$ is $\langle g \rangle$. If $G = \langle g \rangle$ is cyclic, then every subgroup $H \le G$ is cyclic as well, while $|g^s| = \frac{|G|}{\gcd (s, |G|)}$. These can be proven with modular arithmetics.

How to generalise the idea of cyclic? Now, we allow a group to be generated by a set of elements $S$, denoted by $G = \langle S \rangle := \{ g_1^{m_1} g_2^{m_2} \cdots g_n^{m_n} : n \in \mathbb{N}, a_i \in S, m_i \in \mathbb{Z} \}$, noting $S$ is not necessarily finite. When $S$ is finite however, we call $G$ finitely generated. For instances, we can generate $S_n$ with $(1 \; 2)$ and $(1 \; 2 \; \cdots \; n)$, while $D_n = \langle r, s \rangle$ straight from definitions.

Coset, Normal Subgroup and Quotient Group

Given $H \le G$, we define $aH = \{ ah : h \in H \}$ be the coset of $H$ and the equivalence relation $a \sim_{L} b \iff a^{-1} b \in H$, which is reflexive, commutative and transitive. Therefore, the set of left cosets $L_H = \{ aH : a \in G \}$ forms a partition on $G$! We call the cardinality of $L_H$ be the index $[G:H] = |G| / |H|$.

Would $L_H$ be a group as well? Given that we reuse the binary operator of $G$, we would require $aH \cdot bH = (a \cdot b) H$ for all $a, b \in G$, inheriting the definition of $\cdot$ in $G$ naturally, so this means $\{ a h_1 b h_2 : h_1, h_2 \in H \} = \{ a b h : h \in H \}$ for all $a, b \in G$. When this holds, we write $H \trianglelefteq G$, or $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$. There are actually other equivalent criteria of being a normal subgroup, such as $aH = Ha$ for all $a \in G$, $aH \subseteq Ha$ for all $a \in G$, $aHa^{-1} \subseteq H$ for all $a \in G$, $aHa^{-1} = H$ for all $a \in G$, which all these can be proven with algebraic manipulations. When $L_H$ forms a group as well, we call it the quotient group $G / H$. For example, $(\mathbb{Z}, +) \triangleleft (\mathbb{Q}, +) \triangleleft (\mathbb{R}, +) \triangleleft (\mathbb{C}, +)$ and $n \mathbb{Z} \triangleleft \mathbb{Z}$.

Group Homomorphism

The next thing that interests us is the mapping between different groups. Our concern is that the homomorphism should preserve some structural information with minimal requirement. As such, we define a group homomorphism between two groups $G$ and $G'$ as $\phi : G \rightarrow G'$ where $\phi(a \cdot b) = \phi(a) \ast \phi(b)$ for all $a, b \in G$. When $\phi$ is bijective as well, we call $\phi$ an isomorphism and that $G \cong G'$ are isomorphic. We may naturally deduce that $\phi(e_G) = e_{G'}$ and $\phi(g^n) = \phi(g)^n$ directly from definition.

We can further define the image $\text{im}\, \phi := \phi(G) = \{ \phi(g) : g \in G \}$ and the kernel $\ker \phi := \phi^{-1}(e_{G'}) = \{ g \in G: \phi(g) = e_{G'} \}$, analogous to the case of functions. Turns out normality is preserved under homomorphism: $N \trianglelefteq G \implies \phi(N) \trianglelefteq \text{im}\, \phi$, $N' \trianglelefteq G' \implies \phi^{-1}(N') \trianglelefteq G$, which further implies $\ker \phi \trianglelefteq G$. On the other hand, the condition of being cyclic and abelian are also preserved for surjective $\phi$.

The simplest homomorphism is the canonical projection from a group $G$ to its quotient group $G / N$ with $N \trianglelefteq G$, that is we define $\pi_N : G \rightarrow G / N$ where $a \mapsto aN = \bar a$. Clearly this is surjective with kernel $\ker \pi_N = N$. We can deduce the first isomorphism theorem henceforth which states that $G / \ker \phi \cong \text{im}\, \phi$ by introducing the isomorphism $\bar \phi : G / \ker \phi \rightarrow \text{im}\, \phi$ with $\bar \phi (a \ker \phi) = \phi(a)$, $\phi = \bar \phi \circ \pi$. Intuitively speaking, $\ker \phi$ is the set "ignored" by $\phi$, so taking quotient with the kernel results to a group isomorphic to the image.

Structural Theorem of Finite Abelian Group

Turns out we can classify finite abelian groups quite well. First note that $\mathbb{Z}_m \times \mathbb{Z}_n = \mathbb{Z}_{mn}$ if $\gcd(m, n) = 1$. Interestingly, every finite abelian group $G$ is isomorphic to direct product of cyclic groups, that is, $G \cong \prod_{i = 1}^{k} (\mathbb{Z}_{p_i^{n_{i1}}} \times \mathbb{Z}_{p_i^{n_{i2}}} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_{p_i^{n_{i{l_i}}}})$. For example, for a abelian group of cardinality $36$, it must be isomorphic to one of $\mathbb{Z}_6 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$, $\mathbb{Z}_{12} \times \mathbb{Z}_3$, $\mathbb{Z}_{18} \times \mathbb{Z}_3$ or $\mathbb{Z}_{36}$. This would be proved somewhere later on.
