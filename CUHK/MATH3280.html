MATH3280 Introductory Probability

Probability is the art of dealing with uncertainty. We wish to handle probability gracefully using an axiomatic approach, so everything can be well-defined and computed at ease.

Side Note: seems like most notations enlisted here are non-standard. Ingest with a pint of salt.

Probability Space

Consider any experiment where the outcome is uncertain. The sample space \( \Omega \) is defined as the set of all possible outcomes, where any subset \( E \subset \Omega \) is called as an event. With events, we may introduce notations such as \( E \cup F \) (union), \( E \cap F \) or equivalently \( EF \) (intersection), \( \bigcup_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( \bigcap_{n = 1}^{\infty} E_n \), \( E \subset F \) (subset) and \( E^c \) (complement), which essentially just set notations.

Axioms of Probability

How can we define probability for events? One of the ways is perhaps just say we define \( n(E) \) be the number of times the event \( E \) occurs within \( n \) experiments, then take \( P(E) := \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n(E)}{n} \). However, this is not elementary enough, since we haven't prove the convergence yet! It would be obviously better if we may resort to simpler axioms.

Axiomatically, probability is defined as a function $P$ that maps from event space to \( \mathbb{R} \), where

  1. For every event \( E \), \( 0 \le P(E) \le 1 \),
  2. \( P(\Omega) = 1 \) for sample space \( \Omega \),
  3. For countable (possibly infinite) collection \( \{ E_i \} \) of mutually exclusive events, we have \( P(\bigcup E_i) = \sum P(E_i)\).

With these alone, we may deduce interesting stuff like the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle, which states that \[ P\left(\bigcup E_i\right) = \sum_{S \subset [n]} (-1)^{|S|} \bigcap_{j \in S} E_j \] Which can be proven with induction (or fancier stuff).

There are some trivial facts: First, when the number of outcomes is finite and each outcome has the same probability, then the probability of an event can be computed via simply counting outcomes. Second, for monotone sequence of events \( \{ E_i \} \), we have \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(E_n) = P\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} E_n\right) \]

Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is introduced when we try to consider stuff like "assume event \( F \) already happened, what is the probability of event \( E \) happens as well". We denote this conditional probability as \( P(E | F) \), and we trivially have \[ P(E | F) = \frac{P(E \cap F)}{P(F)} \] whenever \( P(F) \gt 0 \). We can chain conditional probability together in the form \[ P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) P(B | A) P(C | A \cap B)\]

One of the key concepts revolving conditional probability is the Baye's Formula: Let's say we already know for events \( E \) and \( F \) the probabilities \( P(E) \), \( P(F|E^c) \) and \( P(F|E) \), what would the conditional probability \( P(E | F) \) be? You may imagine \( E \) represents a hypothesis and \( F \) represents an test; then we would want to know what the probability that the hypothesis holds is, given that the test returns positive.

The Baye's Formula or the law of total probability is simply \[ P(F) = \sum_i P(E_i) \cdot P(F|E_i) \] where \( \{ E_i \} \) partitions the sample space \( \Omega \). With this, we can compute \( P(E | F) \) as \[ P(E | F) = \frac{P(F | E)P(E)}{P(F | E)P(E) + P(F | E^c)P(E^c)}\] Which is just simple algebraic manipulations.

Independent Events

Intuitively, events are independent when they are "irrelevant" from each other. Unfortunately, "irrelevant" is more of a concept about the event itself, and we would like to define independence using probabilities alone. How can we do that?

Formally speaking, we say a collection of events \( \{ E_i \}_{i \in \mathcal{A}} \) is independent iff for all finite \( \mathcal{J} \subset \mathcal{A}\), we have \[ P\left(\bigcap_{j \subset \mathcal{J}} E_j\right) = \prod_{j \in \mathcal{J}} P(E_j) \] Note that this also means that \( E_i \) and any event constructed from \( \{ E_j \}_{j \in \mathcal{A} \setminus \{ i \} } \) via union, intersection and complement is independent.

When the events are identical and independent, we often call the events as trials as in experiement. Those are pretty interesting to analyse and deserve a topic on them own.

Random Variables

Sometimes, we wish to assign values to each of the outcome, say sum of the dots on the dice. We may introduce the concept of random variables as \( X : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \), a real-valued function with sample space as the domain. The image of \( X \) is denoted as \( \Omega_X \). When \( \Omega_X \) is at most countable, then we call \( X \) discrete, and continuous otherwise. Examples of random variables include the indicator variable \( 1_A \) of an event, which takes value \( 1 \) when event \( A \) occurs, and \( 0 \) otherwise.

The treatment for discrete and continuous random variables are slightly different, due to say the summation of uncountable positive values is always unbounded. However, the main constructs like expected values and variance are the same concept, as demonstrated below.

Discrete Random Variables

What if the random variable \( X \) is discrete? In such case, we may define the probability mass function, or PMF, as \[ p(a) = P\{ X = a \} \] for all \( a \in \Omega_X \). Obviously for \( S_X = \{ x_i \} \) countable, we have \( p(x_i) \ge 0 \) and \( \sum p(x_i) = 1 \) since the events \( \{ X = x_i \} \) form a partition of \( \Omega \).

We may define the cumulative distribution function, or CDF, as simply \[ F(a) = \sum_{x \le a} p(x) \] Obviously \( F \) is a step function.

Expected Values and Variance

What can we infer from the PMF \( p \)? First, the expected value or expectation, \( E[X] \) is defined as \[ E[X] = \sum_{x} x \cdot p(x) \] Which intuitively is the weighted average of the random variable \( X \). (Note: I believe the definition \( E[X] = \sum_{s \in \Omega} p(s) \cdot P(\{s\}) \) is wrong, since \( \Omega \) can be uncountable)

For any function \( g : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \), obviously \( g(X) = g \circ X : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \) is a random variable as well. Clearly \[ E[g(X)] = \sum_{x} g(x) \cdot p(x) \] To formally prove this, one may introduce another random event \( Y = g(X) \) and manipulate summations. Clearly we have \( E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b \) and \( E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] \), which the proofs are trivial (but rely on the countability of \( \Omega_X \) and \( \Omega_Y \)).

By denoting \( \mu = E[X] \), the variance of \( X \) is defined as \[ \Var(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - \mu^2\] As an indicator of how \( X \) "spreads away" from the mean \( \mu \). We have \( \Var(aX + b) = a^2 \Var(X) \) via simple computations, and the standard deviation \( \sigma_X = \SD(X) \) is defined as \( \sqrt{\Var(X)} \), which is well-defined since \( \Var(X) \) is always positive.

Standard Examples

There are four examples of random variables that are frequently used: Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson and Geometric.

First, Bernoulli. Suppose the outcome of an experiment is either success or failure, and consider the random variable \( X \) where \( X = 1 \) when success and \( X = 0 \) when failure. If the probability of success is \( p \), then we have the PMF of \( X \) as \[ p(x) = \begin{cases} p & \text{if } x = 1 \\ 1 - p & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases} \] We denote this as \( X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p) \). Obviously we have \( E[X] = p \) and \( \Var(X) = p(1 - p) \).

Second, Binomial. What if we repeat the Bernoulli trial for \( n \) times, and let the random variable \( X \) refers to the number of successes? Obviously we have \( P\{ X = r \} = \binom{n}{r} p^r (1 - p)^{n - r} \), so the PMF of \( X \) is \[ p(r) = \binom{n}{r} p^r (1 - p)^{n - r} \text{ for } r = 0, \dots, n \] We denote this as \( X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \). With linearity of expected value and variance (of independent events), we have \( E[X] = np \) and \( \Var(X) = np(1 - p) \).

Third, Poisson. The binomial one is somewhat complicated to compute - turns out we can approximate it pretty well simply when \( n \) is large, \( p \) is small and \( \lambda = np \) moderate. We have \( P\{ X = r \} = \frac{n^{\underline{r}}}{r!} \left( \frac{\lambda}{n - \lambda}\right)^r \left( 1 - \frac{\lambda}{n} \right)^n \approx e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^r}{r!} \), and so we may consider the PMF where \[ p(r) = e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^r}{r!} \text{ for } r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\] as an approximation of \( \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), and denotes as \( \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \). Being a limiting case of binomial, we naturally have \( E[X] = \Var(X) = \lambda \), which can be shown via simple computations (or, well, generating functions; its gf is \( e^{\lambda(x - 1)} \)).

Finally, Geometric. This random variable is essentially the number of trials needed if we repeat until succeed. If the success probability is \( p \), we clearly have \( P\{ X = r \} = p(1 - p)^{r - 1} \) for \( r \ge 1 \), and so the PMF is \[ p(r) = p(1 - p)^{r - 1} \text{ for } r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\] This is denoted as \( \text{Geometric}(p) \), where \( E[X] = \frac{1}{p} \) and \( \Var(X) = \frac{1 - p}{p^2} \). (Its gf is \( \frac{px}{1 - (1 - p) x}\).)

Continuous Random Variables

A continuous random variable \( X: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \) is such where for all measurable set \( B \), we have \[ P\{ X \in B \} = \int_B f(x) \, dx \] for some function \( f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \), which is called the probability density function or PDF (reference to physics). With such assumption, by taking \( B = [a, b] \) we have \[ P\{ a \le X \le b \} = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \] and so \( P\{ X = a \} = 0 \) for any \( a \in \mathbb{R} \), which implies the closeness at the endpoints should not affect the probability. Of course, we require \[ 1 = P\{ -\infty \lt X \lt \infty \} = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(x) \, dx \]

Expected Values and Variances

Naturally, we define the expected value for a continuous random variable \( X \) as \[ E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) \, dx \] Analogous to the discrete case, we may show \[ E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) f(x) \, dx \] Unfortunately, the proof is somewhat complicated and will be omitted here. One of the interesting identity is that for non-negative continuous \( Y \), we have \[ E[Y] = \int_0^\infty P\{Y \gt y\} \, dy \] Since \( \int_0^\infty P\{ Y \gt y \} \, dy = \int_0^\infty \int_y^\infty f_Y(x) \, dx \, dy = \int_0^\infty (\int_0^x 1 \, dy) \cdot f_Y(x) \, dx = \int_0^\infty x f_Y(x) \, dx \) by Fubini (see MATH2020) and the assumption that \( f_Y \) is integrable. We also have \( \Var(X) = E[X^2] - E[X]^2 \) as similar to the discrete case.

Standard Examples

First, the uniform random variables, where the PDF is \[ f(x) = \frac{1}{b - a} \text{ for } a \le x \le b \] where \( a \lt b \in \mathbb{R} \). Clearly we have \( E[X] = \frac{a + b}{2} \) and \( \Var(X) = \frac{(b - a)^2}{12} \) from simple computations. It is quite simple so not much to say about this, and we may denote this as \( X \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b) \).

Second, the normal random variable. The PDF of \( X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2) \) is \[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sigma} \exp \frac{-(x - \mu)^2}{2 \sigma^2}\] This often refers as the bell curve as well, where \( E[X] = \mu \) and \( \Var(X) = \sigma^2 \) just as intended. Clearly if we let \( Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \), then \[ f_Z(z) = \sigma f_X(z \sigma + \mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \exp \frac{-z^2}{2}\] Note the scaling by \( \sigma \) to make sure the integration over \( (-\infty, \infty) \) stays one. We call \( Z \) the standard normal distribution. To show \( \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_Z(z) = 1 \), simply notice that \( \left( \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \frac{-z^2}{2} \, dz \right)^2 = \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_{0}^R 2\pi r \exp \frac{-r^2}{2} \, dr = 2 \pi \). The CDF of \( Z \) is often denoted as \( \Phi(z) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z \exp \frac{-y^2}{2} \, dy \). Essentially, when something is distributed normally, then we may refer to the table of \( \Phi \) and get the probability base on how many standard deviations \( \sigma \) it is off from the mean \( \mu \). For instance, for \( X \sim \text{Normal}(\mu, \sigma^2) \), then \[ P\{ X \le a \} = \Phi \left( \frac{a - \mu}{\sigma} \right) \]

The DeMoivre-Laplace limit theorem states that as \( n \to \infty \) and for \( X_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), we have \[ \frac{X_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}} \rightarrow \text{Normal}(0, 1) \] Noting \( E[X] = np \) and \( \Var(X) = np(1 - p) \). We write \( \{ Z_n \} \rightarrow Z \) when for all \( a \lt b \), we have \( P\{ a \le Z_n \le b \} \to P\{ a \le Z \le b \} \). That is, when the number of bernoulli trials becomes large, it starts to behave like a normal distribution. For example, for \( X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p) \), we can approximate \( P\{ X = np \} \) as \( P \{ np - 0.5 \le X \lt np + 0.5 \} \) (continuity correction). With \( \mu = np \) and \( \sigma = \sqrt{np(1 - p)} \), this is \( \Phi\left(\frac{0.5}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}}\right) - \Phi\left(\frac{-0.5}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}}\right) \).

Finally, we introduce the exponential random variables with PDF \[ f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \text{ for } x \ge 0 \] Which more relevants to like particle decay. For \( X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda) \), we have \( E[X^n] = \int_0^\infty x^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = n \int_0^\infty x^{n - 1} e^{-\lambda x} \, dx = \frac{n}{\lambda} E[X^{n - 1}] = \frac{n!}{\lambda^n} \). Hence, \( E[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda} \) and \( \Var(X) = E[X^2] - E[X]^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \).

Deriving Density Function

Given the density function \( f \) of a continuous random variable \( X \) and some arbitrary \( h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \), how can we find the density function corresponds to \( h(X) \)?

This sounds easy and is easy; the first way is to compute the CDF of \( X \), then compute the CDF of \( h(X) \), and compute back the PDF via differentiation, which is called the method of distribution functions.

The second method is called the method of transformations, which is applicable when \( h \) is differentiable and strictly monotonic (and so invertible), which then \[ f_Y(y) = f_X(h^{-1}(y)) |(h^{-1})'(y)| \] For all \( y \in h(\Omega_X) \), and this is essentially some sort of change-of-variable theorem.

Joint Distribution Functions

Obviously, we can joint random variables together. For random variables \( X \) and \( Y \), we can always define the joint CDF as \( F(a, b) = P\{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \) for \( -\infty \lt a, b \lt \infty \), where \( \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \) just means \( \{ X \le a \} \cap \{ Y \le b \} \). The marginal CDF for \( X \) and \( Y \) respectively can be computed as \( F_X(a) = F(a, \infty) \) and \( F_Y(b) = F(\infty, b) \). More technically, \( F_X(a) = P \{ X = a \} = P \left( \lim_{b \to \infty} \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} \right) = \lim_{b \rightarrow \infty} (P \{ X \le a, Y \le b \} ) = F (a, \infty) \), which we can pass the limit outside because \( P \) is continuous here (as it is a riemann integral).

When both \( X \) and \( Y \) are discrete, we may define the joint PMF as \( p(x, y) = P \{ X = x, Y = y \} \). The marginal PMF can be obtained in a similar manner as above.

How about joint PDF? Unfortunately, it does not always exists: For example, for \( X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1) \) and \( Y = X \), the joint PDF of \( X \) and \( Y \) does not exists, since the joint CDF is zero! We say \( X \) and \( Y \) are jointly continuous when there exists a function \( f(x, y) \) such that for any measurable set \( C \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \), we have \[ P\{ (X, Y) \in C \} = \iint_{C} f(x, y) \, dx \, dy \] Which then we may derive that \( f(a, b) = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial a \partial b} F(a, b) \) and the marginal PDF as \( f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y) \, dy \) and \( f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x, y) \, dx \).

Independent Random Variables

Similar to the case for events (which essentially is binary random variables), we say two random variables \( X \) and \( Y \) are independent whenever for all \( A, B \subset \mathbb{R} \), we have \[ P\{ X \in A, Y \in B \} = P\{ X \in A \} P \{Y \in B \} \] Which is equivalent to \[ P\{ X \le a, Y \le b \} = P\{ X \le a \} P \{ Y \le b \} \] for all \( a, b \in \mathbb{R} \), or equivalently \( F(a, b) = F_X(a) F_Y(b) \). (The proof is non-trivial so is not covered.) In the case of both \( X \) and \( Y \) are discrete, clearly above is equivalent to \( p(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y) \) for all \( x, y \), whereas for the case of jointly continuous the above is equivalent to \( f(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y) \).

Sum of Independent Random Variables

For independent variables \( X \) and \( Y \), we have \( F_{X + Y}(a) = P\{ X + Y \le a \} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F_X(a - y) f_Y(y) \, dy \), which \( F_{X + Y} \) is called the convolution of \( F_X \) and \( F_Y \). Differentiating \( F_{X + Y}(a) \) gives \( f_{X + Y}(a) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(a - y) f_Y(y) \, dy \) naturally. Using this, we may show that for \( X_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1) \) independent and \( F_n(x) = P \{ \sum X_i \le x \} \), then \( F_n(x) = \frac{x^n}{n!} \) when \( 0 \le x \le 1 \).

An interesting corollary is about normal random variables. Suppose \( X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2) \) are independent, then turns out \( \sum X_i \sim \text{Normal}(\sum \mu_i, \sum \sigma_i^2) \). Why is that the case? It suffices to show for the case of two random variables, say \( X \sim \text{Normal}(0, \sigma_X^2) \) and \( Y \sim \text{Normal}(0, \sigma_Y^2) \). Then \( f_{X + Y}(a) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_X(a - y) f_Y(y) \, dy = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \exp \frac{-(a - y)^2}{2 \sigma_X^2} \exp \frac{-y^2}{2 \sigma_Y^2} \, dy \), which means it suffices to show \[ \frac{1}{2 \pi \sigma_X \sigma_Y} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp \frac{-(a - y)^2}{2 \sigma_X^2} \exp \frac{-y^2}{2 \sigma_Y^2} \, dy \] and \[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi (\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2)} } \exp \frac{-a^2}{2(\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2)} \] are equivalent. This is obviously trivial; let \( \sigma_{X + Y} = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2} \), then \( \dfrac{-(a - y)^2}{2\sigma_X^2} + \dfrac{-y^2}{2 \sigma_Y^2} = \dfrac{-(a - y)^2 \sigma_Y^2 - y^2 \sigma_X^2}{2 \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2} = \dfrac{\dfrac{-a^2 \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2}{\sigma_{X + Y}^2} - \left(\dfrac{a \sigma_Y^2}{\sigma_{X + Y}} - y \sigma_{X + Y}\right)^2}{2 \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2} \), and so by offsetting \( y \), we are computing \[ \dfrac{1}{2 \pi \sigma_X \sigma_Y} \exp \frac{-a^2}{2 \sigma_{X + Y}^2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp -\frac{\sigma_{X + Y}^2}{2 \sigma_X^2 \sigma_Y^2} y^2 \, dy \] which by Guassian Integral we are done.
