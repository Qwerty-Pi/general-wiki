Complex Numbers

Introduction

We put our focus on complex numbers \( \mathbb{C} \). What are them? In short, complex numbers is an extension of real numbers such that every real non-constant polynomial has root, "completing" the real numbers \( \R \). We introduce the purely imaginary number \( i \) where \( i^2 = -1 \) or \( i = \sqrt{-1} \), which we can write \( \C = \R[i] = \{ x + yi : x, y \in \R \} \).

For \( z = x + yi \), we denote \( \Re z = x \) the real part of \( z \), and \( \Im z = y \) the imaginary part of \( z \). Clearly, \( z \) is real iff \( \Im z = 0 \), whereas \( z \) is called purely imaginary iff \( \Re z = 0 \). A way to visualise complex numbers is to draw it on a 2D (Argand) plane, which the x-axis is the real axis and the y-axis is the imaginary axis. For \( z = x + yi \), we define the complex conjugate \( \overline{z} = x - yi \) - essentially flipping the complex number across the x-axis.

How about comparison? Given \( z_1, z_2 \in \C \), how could we compare them? We say \( z_1 = z_2 \) iff both \( \Re z_1 = \Re z_2 \) and \( \Im z_1 = \Im z_2 \) holds. However, comparison \( z_1 \lt z_2 \) is not well-defined. We can of course customarily define some sort of order (say distance to origin, tie-break by angle), but that is not going to be very useful in general.

For \( z_1 = x_1 + y_1 i \) and \( z_2 = x_2 + y_2 i \), we may define \[ z_1 \pm z_2 := (x_1 \pm x_2) + (y_1 \pm y_2) i \] And \[ z_1 z_2 := (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2) + (x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1) i \] How about division? We can write \[ (x + yi)^{-1} = \frac{x - yi}{(x + yi) (x - yi)} = \frac{x - yi}{x^2 + y^2}\]

Interestingly, conjugation is "exchangable" with these operators: \[ \conj{z_1 \pm z_2} = \conj{z_1} \pm \conj{z_2} \] \[ \conj{z_1 \times z_2} = \conj{z_1} \times \conj{z_2} \] \[ \conj{z^{-1}} = \conj{z}^{-1}\] One may trivially verify them by computations. Alternatively, for the last two equality, we may use polar coordinates or Euler's formula: From power series expansion, we have \[ e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta \] In other words, \( \rho e^{i \theta} \) represents an anticlockwise of \( \theta \) of the "vector" \( \rho \). We can write \( z = x + yi = \rho e^{i \theta} \), which the conjugate \( \overline{z} \) is just \( \rho e^{-i \theta} \), and \( z^{-1} \) is just \( \rho^{-1} e^{-i \theta} \), whereas \( (\rho_1 e^{i \theta_1}) (\rho_2 e^{i \theta_2}) = (\rho_1 \rho_2) e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}\). The equalities trivially follow. As a side note, we also have the de Moivre's formula \[ (\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)^n = \cos n \theta + i \sin n \theta \] Which is an immediate consequence of the Euler's formula. Essentially - multiply by a complex number \( \rho e^{i \theta} \) is just scaling by \( \rho \) and rotate by angle \( \theta \).

We call \( \rho = |z| = \sqrt{(\Re z)^2 + (\Im z)^2} \) the modulus of \( z \), and \( \theta \) an argument of \( z \). Clearly there are many choices of \( \theta \) off by multiples of \( 2 \pi \), and so we define \( \operatorname{arg} z \) the set of all arguments, and \( \operatorname{Arg} z \) the principle argument within the range \( (-\pi, \pi] \).

As a final note, the triangle inequality \( |z_1| + |z_2| \ge |z_1 + z_2| \) and \( |z_1 - z_2| \ge ||z_1| - |z_2|| \) holds as usual. (What do you expect?)

Geometry in the Complex Plane

An interesting topic for us to explore is - essentially complex plane \( \C \) is the extension of the 2D euclidean plane \( \R^2 \). Can we represent geometric objects in \( \C \) as well?

First of all, we can represent a circle centered at \( z_0 \) with radius \( r_0 \) as \[ \{ z \in \C : |z - z_0| = r_0 \} \] And an ellipse with foci \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \) as \[ \{ z \in \C : |z - z_1| + |z - z_2| = d \} \] Another interesting represnetation of circle is as follows: For a circle passing through \( z_1, z_2 \) and \( z_3 \), we know the locus of the circle is \[ \{ z \in \C : \angle z_1 z_3 z_2 = \angle z_1 z z_2 \}\] What is \( \angle z_1 z z_2 \)? It is just \( \arg \frac{z_2 - z}{z_1 - z} \)! Hence, we have another (more complicated) circle representation \[ C = \left\{ z \in \C : \Im \left[\left(\frac{z_2 - z}{z_1 - z}\right) / \left(\frac{z_2 - z_3}{z_1 - z_3}\right)\right] = 0 \right\} \] Similarly, to represent a line passing through \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \), we simply have \[ L = \left\{ z \in \C : \Im \left(\frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\right) = 0 \right\} \] Clearly any line through \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \) cuts the plane into two half-planes: and the sign of \[ \Im \left( \frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1} \right) \] tells you which side of \( z \) is of with respect to \( \overrightarrow{z_1 z_2} \).

In a sense, we can treat complex numbers not only a vector, but scaling and rotation operator as well - which makes representation of geometric stuff sometimes much easier.

Using this point of view, \( z^n \) is equivalent to apply the same operator \( z \), repeat \( n \) times. Given \( z_0 \), can we find its \( n \)-th root such that \( z^n = z_0 \)? Clearly this is equivalent to \[ \rho^n e^{i n \theta} = \rho_0 e^{i \theta_0} \] Which means the scaling \( \rho \) should be \( \sqrt[n]{\rho_0} \), but the rotation \( \theta \) can be any of \( \frac{\theta_0}{n} + \frac{2 k \pi}{n} \) where \( k \in \Z \), since we can rotate any number of complete circles of \( 2 \pi \). Therefore, for any non-zero \( z_0 \in \C \), there are \( n \) distinct \( n \)-th root of \( z_0 \). Specially, we call the \( n \)-th root of \( 1 \) as the \( n \)-th roots of unity, which exactly is just \[ \left\{ \exp \frac{2k\pi i}{n} : 0 \le k \lt n \right\} \] We call \( \sqrt[n]{\rho_0} e^{\frac{i \theta_0}{n}} \) the principal \( n \)-th root of \( z \), since we are using the principal argument here.

Topology of Complex Numbers

Topologically, we define an open ball of radius \( r_0 \) centered at \( z_0 \) as \[ B_{r_0}(z_0) = \{ z \in \C : |z - z_0| \lt r_0 \}\] Given any set \( S \) and any point \( z \), intuitively, we can classify \( z \) as one of the three cases: (1) \( z \) is completely inside \( S \), (2) \( z \) is completely outside \( S \), and (3) \( z \) is on the boundary of \( S \). How can we capture those intuition? With open balls.

We say \( z \) is (1) interior of \( S \) or \( z \in \Int S \) if there exists \( r \gt 0 \) where \( B_r(z) \subset S \); (2) exterior of \( S \) or \( z \in \Ext S \) if there exists \( r \gt 0 \) where \( B_r(z) \subset S^{c} \); (3) boundary of \( S \) (limit point, accumulation point) or \( z \in \partial S \) if neither of the above cases happen. We call the closure of \( S \) as \( \overline{S} = S \cup \partial S \). We say \( S \) is open iff \( S = \Int S \), and is closed iff \( S = \Int S \cup \partial S \). In plain text, \( S \) is open whenever it doesn't contain any of the boundary point, and is closed whenever it contains all of the boundary point. Note that open and closed are related, but not opposite properties - for example, \( \varnothing \) and \( \C \) are both open and closed, and \( \{ z \in \C : 1 \lt |z| \le 2 \} \) is neither open nor closed.

We say \( S \subset \C \) is bounded iff there exists an open ball \( B_r(0) \supset S \). For bounded \( S \), we can define its diameter as \[ \operatorname{diam} S = \sup \{ |z_1 - z_2| : z_1, z_2 \in S \}\] We also say \( S \) is connected iff we cannot partition \( S = S_1 \cup S_2 \) where \( S_1 \subset U \), \( S_2 \subset V \) for disjoint open sets \( U \) and \( V \). An open cover of \( S \) is defined as a family of open sets \( \{ O_\alpha \} \) where \( S \in \bigcup_{\alpha} O_\alpha \).

We say a set \( S \) is compact iff any open cover of \( S \) admits a finite subcover. Turns out in \( \C \), this is equivalent to \( S \) is closed and bounded! (This is going to be explained somewhere else, so this will not be proven here.) Interestingly, given an infinite descending chain \( S_1 \supset S_2 \supset \cdots \supset S_n \supset \cdots \) with \( \operatorname{diam}(S_n) \rightarrow 0 \) of compact sets, there exists a unique \( z_0 \in \C \) such that \( z_0 \in \bigcap S_n \). Uniqueness is easy to prove, whereas existence is implied by picking a point \( z_n \in S_n \), then \( z = \lim z_n \) (well-defined by Cauchy). If \( z \not\in S_N \) for some \( N \), then there exists an open ball \( B_\varepsilon(z) \) that is disjoint with \( S_N \) (since \( S_n \) are compact), contradicting the fact that \( z \) is a limit point.

Complex Functions

Okay, enough open ball knowledge. We can define complex functions normally as \( f: S_1 \subset \C \rightarrow S_2 \subset \C \), whereas \( f \) can also be represented as \( f = f_1 + f_2 i \) with \( f_1, f_2 : S_1 \rightarrow \R \). One question maybe - what is the difference of this compare to \( f: \R^2 \rightarrow \R^2 \), then? We can replicate every operations done in \( \C \) to \( \R^2 \) anyways! For example, for \( f(z) = z^2 \), we have \( f(x + yi) = (x^2 - y^2) + 2xyi \). The discrepency will appear when we try to do integration and derivatives.

Clearly we can define functions as \( z \mapsto z + z_0 \) (translation), \( z \mapsto z e^{i \theta} \) (rotation), \( z \mapsto r_0 z \) (scaling) and \( z \mapsto \conj{z} \) (reflection across real-axis). We can also define the exponential function \( z \mapsto e^{z} \) over \( \C \). We can define \[ \cos z := \frac{e^{iz} + e^{-iz}}{2} \qquad \sin z := \frac{e^{iz} - e^{-iz}}{2i}\] And \[ \cosh z := \frac{e^z + e^{-z}}{2} \qquad \sinh z := \frac{e^z - e^{-z}}{2} \] As the complex analogue of sine and cosine functions. Essentially those are just derived from their corresponding power series.

How about complex logarithm? To solve \( e^z = \rho e^{i \theta} \), we clearly have \( z = \log \rho + i (\theta + 2k \pi) \) for any \( k \in \Z \). To make logarithm a single-valued function, we can pick any branch as \( \alpha \in \R \) and pick the \( \theta + 2k \pi \in ( \alpha, \alpha + 2\pi] \). For the principal branch, we pick \( \alpha = - \pi \), which means we pick the unique \( \theta \in ( - \pi, \pi] \). In other words, \( \log z := \log |z| + i \Arg z \), a continuous function defined on the open set \[ U = \{ z \in \C : |z| \gt 0, -\pi \lt \Arg z \lt \pi \} \] Given any branch of logarithm, we can define the complex power function \( z^c \) as well, as \( z^c = e^{c \log z} \).

Limit and Continuity

Essentially, the concept of limit just follows that of multi-variable calculus: Given \( f : \Omega \to \C \) where \( \Omega \) open, if \( z_0 \) is accumulation point of \( \Omega \setminus \{ z_0 \} \), then we write \[ \lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = w_0 \] If \( |f(z) - w_0| \lt \varepsilon \) for all \( z \in \Omega \) and \( 0 \lt |z - z_0| \lt \delta \). We say \( f \) is continuous at \( z_0 \in \Omega \) if \[ \lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0) \] If \( f \) is continuous at every \( z \in \Omega \), we say \( f \) is continuous on \( \Omega \). Clearly continuity is preserved under addition, multiplication, division and composition.

Differentiability

How about differentiability? We say \( f \) is differentiable at \( z_0 \in \Omega \) if \[ \lim_{z \rightarrow z_0} \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \] Exists, which is denoted as \( f'(z_0) \). Most things just repeat that of multivariable calculus, so just note that if we write \( f = u + i v \), even if both \( u \) and \( v \) are differentiable in \( \R^2 \), does not imply \( f \) is differentiable in \( \C \)!

Intuitively, we require \( f_x = u_x + i v_x \) and \( i^{-1} f_y = -i (u_y + i v_y) \) to be equal - which implies \( u_x = v_y \) and \( u_y = -v_x \). This is the Cauchy-Riemann Equations. On the other hand, if both \( u \) and \( v \) are differentiable at \( z_0 \in \Omega \) and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann Equations, then \( f'(z_0) = u_x(z_0) + i u_y(z_0) \). This is due to the linear approximations \( u(z) = u(z_0) + \nabla u (z_0) \cdot (z - z_0) + \varepsilon_u(z) \) where \( \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{\varepsilon_u(z)}{|z - z_0|} = 0 \), which we can just expand trivially to show the linear approximation for \( f \) as well.

For \( f : \Omega \subset \C \to \C \), we say \( f \) is analytic at \( z_0 \) if \( f \) is differentiable on \( B_{\varepsilon}(z_0) \subset \Omega \) for some \( \varepsilon \gt 0 \). If \( f \) is analytic at every point \( z \in \Omega \), we say \( f \) is analytic on \( \Omega \). Specially, if \( \Omega = \C \), we say \( f \) is an entire function.

Now, there is a bunch of "random facts". First of all, an open set \( \Omega \) is connected iff it is polygonally connected: for any \( z_1, z_2 \in \Omega \), there exists a polygonal line of finitely many segments in \( \Omega \) connecting \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \). Remember the original definition of connectedness is there doesn't exist an partition of \( \Omega \) that are included in two disjoint open sets. The proof is quite interesting - let \( S \) be the set of all \( z \in \Omega \) where \( z \) and \( z_0 \) are polygonally connected. Then \( \Omega \setminus S \) is also open, forcing \( S = \Omega \) since \( \Omega \) is connected.

Now, if \( \Omega \) is connected and \( f' : \Omega \to \C \) is zero on \( \Omega \), then \( f \) is constant on \( \Omega \) since for any \( z_1, z_2 \in \Omega \), we can just use the polyline to show \( f \) is constant for each line segment. Interestingly, we may use this to prove if both \( f \) and \( \conj{f} \) are differentiable on open connected \( \Omega \), then \( f \) is a constant: since by Cauchy-Riemann we must have \( f' = 0 \) on \( \Omega \).

Next, if \( f = u + iv \) is analytic on open set \( \Omega \), then both \( u \) and \( v \) are harmonic (\( u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\)). This is once again trivial by Cauchy-Riemann.

Complex Integral

To integrate \( w : [a, b] \to \C \) where \( w(t) = u(t) + i v(t) \), trivially we have \[ \int_a^b w(t) \, dt = \int_a^b u(t) \, dt + i \int_a^b v(t) \, dt \] A parametrised curve \( \gamma \) is a set \( \{ z = z(t) = x(t) + y(t) i : t \in [a, b] \} \) where \( x(t), y(t) \) are continuous. We say the curve is smooth if \( z \) is \( \mathcal{C}^1 \). We can then define the line integral of \( \gamma \) as \[ \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = \int_a^b f(z(t)) z'(t) \, dt \] Which we can change accordingly if \( \gamma \) is only piecewise smooth. One inequality to note is \[ \left| \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz \right| \le \sup_{z \in \gamma} |f(z)| \cdot \operatorname{length}(\gamma) \] Which is trivial, but will be useful later.

Now, let's move on to more interesting parts. First of all, consider an open connected set \( \Omega \). If \( f \) is continuous and \( F' = f \) on \( \Omega \), then for any piecewise smooth curve \( \gamma \) from \( z_1 \) to \( z_2 \) where \( z_1, z_2 \in \Omega \), we have \[ \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = F(z_2) - F(z_1) \] In particular, integral of \( f \) along any piecewise-smooth closed curve is 0. In fact, the opposite implication holds as well! The third equivalent condition is that the integral is independent of paths.

Okay, enough introduction - the above are just identical to those in euclidean space! What are the things that are specific to complex analysis?

Complex Analysis

Cauchy-Goursat Theorem

The theorem is actually very simple: if \( f \) is analytic in all points interior and on a simple closed curve \( \gamma \), then \[ \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 0 \] Normally, we require \( f \) to have an anti-derivative. But here the condition is rather whether \( f \) is analytic - differentiable over an neighbourhood (open set) of each point. Also, we require \( \gamma \) to be simple, that is, no intersection is allowed. Why does this magically holds?

We first consider the case where \( \gamma = \gamma_0 \) encloses a rectangle. By repeatedly cut \( \gamma_n \) into four identical smaller rectangles and pick the maximum integral rectangle, we have a descending chain of rectangles \[ R_0 \supset R_1 \supset \cdots \supset R_n \supset \cdots \] where \( \gamma_n \) encloses \( R_n \), and \[ \left| \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz \right| \le 4^n \left| \int_{\gamma_n} f(z) \, dz \right| \] Since \( f(z_0) \) and \( f'(z_0)(z - z_0) \) has anti-derivative, magically, we have \[ \begin{align*} & \int_{\gamma_n} f(z) \, dz \\ = & \int_{\gamma_n} [f(z) - f(z_0) - f'(z_0) (z - z_0)] \, dz \\ = & \int_{\gamma_n} \left[ \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} - f'(z_0) \right] (z - z_0) \, dz \end{align*} \] Which the proof trivially follows by the fact that \( f \) is analytic at \( z_0 \).

Another special case - what if \( \Omega \) is a open disc, and \( f \) is analytic on \( \Omega \)? We can actually show \[ \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 0 \] for all closed curve \( \gamma \) in \( \Omega \) (no need to be simple). We may simply use Cauchy-Goursat for rectangles to obtain an anti-derivative, then the rest follows.

Finally, the main theorem. The proof is remarkably simple: Simply repeatedly "shrink" the simple closed curves \( \{ \gamma_0, \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_n, \dots \} \) such that \( \operatorname{length}(\gamma_n) \rightarrow 0 \) and \[ \int_{\gamma_n} f(z) \, dz = \int_{\gamma_{n + 1}} f(z) \, dz \] By cutting the "strip" between \( \gamma_n \) and \( \gamma_{n + 1} \) into pieces in small discs, then the proof trivially follows.

For simply connected (opposite: multiply connected) \( \Omega \) where every simple closed curve enclose only points in \( \Omega \), we can prove a stronger result. If \( f \) is analytic on \( \Omega \), then for any closed curve \( \gamma \) we have \( \int_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 0 \). (I didn't find a simple proof to this? "Subtlities occur when the curve intersects with itself infinitely many times" - perhaps can prove with convergence) For multiply connected domain, we can "encircle" the holes with simple closed curves, then the theorem holds similarly.

Cauchy Integral Formula

The Cauchy Integral Formula is related to the Cauchy-Goursat Theorem, but asks the question - if the function \( \frac{f(z)}{z - z_0} \) where \( f \) analytic has a "pole" at \( z_0 \), what is its line integral encircling the pole?

Formally speaking, let \( \Omega \) open, enclosed by simple closed curve \( \gamma \) with counterclockwise orientation. Then if \( f \) is analytic on \( \overline{\Omega} \) (closure), then for any \( z_0 \in \Omega \), we have \[ f(z_0) = \frac{1}{2 \pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z - z_0} \, dz \] The proof is simple - By Cauchy-Goursat, we can pass \( \gamma \) to \( C_\rho \) without changing the integral for some \( \rho \gt 0 \) where \( C_\rho \) is the circle centered at \( z_0 \) with radius \( \rho \), since \( f \) is analytic on the closure of \( \gamma \cup -C_\rho \). How to, then, evaluate \[ \oint_{C_\rho} \frac{f(z)}{z - z_0} \, dz \] We may use a similar trick appear before - We rewrite it into the form \[ \oint_{C_\rho} \left[ \frac{f(z) - f(z_0)}{z - z_0} + \frac{f(z_0)}{z - z_0} \right] \, dz \] The first term is bounded on \( \Omega \setminus \{ z_0 \} \) (note: I think in order to prove this, we need to split into two cases, \( |z - z_0| \ge \delta \) and \( |z - z_0| \lt \delta \)), and so as \( \rho \rightarrow 0 \), this term tends to \( 0 \) as well. The second term is just \( 2 \pi i f(z_0) \), and the equality holds.

We can, in fact, prove a more general statement. Under the same assumption, we can show \( f \) is differentiable of all orders, and for any \( n \ge 0 \), \[ \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!} = \frac{1}{2 \pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{(z - z_0)^{n + 1}} \, dz \] The left side is very Taylor, but we will ignore it for now. We can simply proceed by induction, and evaluate the value of \[ \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0 + h) - f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!} \right] \] by the first principles.

We can indeed explain a bit on why it looks like this. Let's assume \( f \) can be written as the form of power series \[ f(z) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{f^{(k)}(z_0)}{k!} (z - z_0)^k \] Which converges within some radius from \( z_0 \). Then \[ \frac{f(z)}{(z - z_0)^{n + 1}} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{f^{(k)}(z_0)}{k!} (z - z_0)^{k - n - 1} \] But \[ \oint_\gamma (z - z_0)^n = \begin{cases} 2 \pi i & \text{ if } n = -1 \\ 0 & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases} \] Since except for \( n = -1 \), others all have anti-derivative on \( \Omega \setminus \{ z_0 \} \).

Note that generalised Cauchy Integral Formula implies if \( f \) is analytic at \( z_0 \), then it is differentiable of all orders at \( z_0 \)! There is an interesting consequence to this - the Morera's Theorem states that for continuous \( f \) on open connected set \( \Omega \), if \( \oint_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 0 \) for all closed curves in \( \Omega \), then \( f \) is differentiable on \( \Omega \). The existence of anti-derivative \( F \) implies \( F \) is analytic, and so \( F \) has all order of derivatives, so as \( f = F' \).

Cauchy's Inequality

Cauchy's Inequality is an immediate corollary of Cauchy Integral Formula - suppose \( f \) is differentiable on an open set containing \( \overline{B_R(z_0)} \) and \( \gamma = C_R(z_0) \), then we have \[ \left| \frac{f^{(n)} (z_0)}{n!} \right| \le \frac{\max_\gamma |f(z)|}{R^n} \]

Turns out we can prove quite crazy result with Cauchy's Inequality! For example, when \( f \) is analytic on \( \C \) (in other words, entire) and bounded, then \[ |f'(z_0)| \le \frac{\max_{C_R} f(z)}{R} \le \frac{M}{R} \] Which is forced to be \( 0 \) as \( R \to \infty \). Hence, \( f \) must be constant. This is the Liouville's Theorem.

In turns, we can prove the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra with this - suppose we have a non-constant polynomial \( P \in \C[z] \). If \( P(z) \) does not have any root, then \( \frac{1}{P(z)} \) is well-defined on \( \C \) and is entire. We can show \( \frac{1}{P(z)} \) is bounded by case-handling \( |z| \gt R \) or \( |z| \le R \) for some sufficiently large \( R \), and so by Liouville we have \( \frac{1}{P(z)} \) being constant, contradiction!

Finally, we have the Maximum Modulus Principle - for non-constant \( f \) being analytic on open connected \( \Omega \), no \( z_0 \in \Omega \) has maximum modulus. We can first consider the disc case: Suppose \( |f(z)| \le |f(z_0)| \) for all \( z \in B_R(z_0) \). By Cauchy, we will need \( |f(z)| = |f(z_0)| \), and so the \( |f(z)| \) is constant in \( B_R(z_0) \). Since the domain is (polygonally) connected, \( |f| \) is constant on \( \Omega \), and so \( f \) is constant as well(!)

By considering \( f^{-1} \) (non-zero on \( \Omega \)), \( e^{f(z)} \) and \( e^{-if(z)} \), we can extend the theorem to minimum of \( |f(z)| \), the real and imaginary parts of \( f \) as well.

Complex Power Series

Series

A series is a partial sum of the form \[ \sum_{n = 0}^\infty z_n \] When is this well-defined? Of course, when the sum converges to some value. We say the series absolutely converges if \[ \sum_{n = 0}^\infty |z_n| \] exists as well. Trivially, we have absolutely convergence implies convergence (using, say, Cauchy's criteria).

Power Series

Given a power series \( \mathcal{F}(z) = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty a_n z^n \), for what \( z \) does \( \mathcal{F} \) converges? Turns out the condition is nice - consider the radius of convergence \[ R = \left( \operatorname{\overline{lim}}_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{a_n} \right)^{-1} \] Then \( \mathcal{F} \) converges absolutely for \( |z| \lt R \), and diverges for \( |z| \gt R \), where \( B_R(0) \) is called the disc of convergence. The proof is just comparing against a geometric series - say for the case \( |z| \lt R \), simply note that there exists some \( N \) such that \( |a_n z^n| \le \rho^n \) for all \( n \ge N \) for some \( \rho \lt 1 \), and the case of \( |z| \gt R \) is analogous.

Interestingly - if we differentiate a power series, it still has the same radius of convergence! For \( \mathcal{F}(z) = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty a_n z^n \), we actually intuitively have \[ \mathcal{F}'(z) = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty n a_n z^{n - 1} \] Simply note that \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \sqrt[n]{n} = 1 \) and we can cut the tails of \( \mathcal{F}' \) and \( \mathcal{F} \) such that the absolute value of the tails is sufficiently small, which the derivative of the head matches.

Taylor Series

Here comes the Taylor Series - suppose \( f \) is analytic on \( B_R(z_0) \), then \( f \) can be written as \[ f(z) = \sum_{n \ge 0} a_n (z - z_0)^n \] For all \( z \in B_R(z_0) \) where \[ a_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!} \] When \( z_0 = 0 \), we call this the Maclaurin Series. To prove this, without loss of generality, assume \( z_0 = 0 \). Let \( \gamma = C_{\frac{R + |z|}{2}} \), then by Cauchy, \[ f(z) = \oint_\gamma \frac{f(w)}{w - z} \, dw \] We want to make the pole at \( z_0 = 0 \) instead, that is, the numerator should be \( w^k \). Simply note that \[ \frac{1}{w - z} = \frac{1}{w} + \cdots + \frac{z^{n - 1}}{w^{n}} + \frac{z^n}{w^n (w - z)}\] And so the prefix becomes exactly the Taylor coefficients, again by generalised Cacuhy Integral Formula. The final term can be shown to be tends to \( 0 \) as \( n \to \infty \) trivially.

Laurent Series

Now we allow holes. Suppose \( f \) is analytic on an annulus \( \Omega := B_{R_2}(z_0) \setminus \overline{B_{R_1}(z_0)} \), then for any \( z \in \Omega \) we have \[ f(z) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^{\infty} c_n (z - z_0)^n \] With \[ c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f(w)}{(w - z_0)^{n + 1}} \, dw \] This is obviously an interesting extension of the Taylor Series. Clearly if \( f \) is analytic in \( \overline{B_{R_1}(z_0)} \) as well, then \( c_n = 0 \) for any \( n \lt 0 \) via Cauchy-Goursat Theorem.

We can show the coefficient of Laurent Series are unique - suppose \[ \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty c_n (z - z_0)^n = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty \tilde{c_n} (z - z_0)^n \] Since both are absolutely convergent, by integrating \[ \oint_\gamma (z - z_0)^n \, dz \] We may filter the coefficients \( c_{-1} \). The proof for Laurent Series is just apply Cauchy-Goursat for multi-connected domains, then prove the same way as the proof for Taylor Series.

Singularities

Assume \( f \) is analytic on a punctured disc \( B_R(z_0) \setminus \{ z_0 \} \) for some \( R \). We can write its Laurent Series out as \[ f(z) = \sum_{n = -\infty}^\infty c_n (z - z_0)^n \] Obviously, we want to classify the type of the puncture: Consider \( m = \inf \{ n \in \Z : c_n \ne 0 \} \). If (1) \( m \ge 0 \), we say \( z_0 \) a removable singularity; If (2) \( -\infty \lt m \lt 0 \), we say \( z_0 \) a pole of order \( -m \); If (3) \( m = -\infty \), we say \( z_0 \) an essential singularity.

The naming captures the nature of the singularities - which is, how hard is it for us to "complete" the function \( f \) at \( z_0 \).

For removable singularity, we simply take \( f(z_0) = c_0 \), which makes it analytic over its disc of convergence. Interestingly, if \( f \) is bounded and analytic over \( B_R(z_0) \setminus \{ z_0 \} \), it has removable singularity at \( z_0 \) as well - which makes sense because \( |f| \) would be unbounded near \( z_0 \) otherwise. On the other hand, we can simply use \( \gamma = C_\rho(z_0) \) for some sufficiently small \( \rho \), and just compute \( c_n \) directly.

For poles of order \( m \), we can show \( |f(z)| \to \infty \) as \( z \to z_0 \). The proof is rather trivial by simply "offset" the Laurent Series to make it of the form \( \frac{g(z)}{z^m} \), where \( g(z) \to c_{-m} \) and \( z^m \to 0 \) as \( z \to c_0 \).

Finally, for essential singularity, we can show for any \( c \in \C \), there exists a sequence \( z_k \to z_0 \) such that \( |f(z_k) - c| \to 0 \) as \( k \to \infty \). Suppose otherwise that for some \( \delta \gt 0 \) we have \( |f(z) - c| \gt \varepsilon \) for all \( z \in B_\delta(z_0) \setminus \{ z_0 \} \). Then \( g(z) := \frac{1}{f(z) - c} \) is analytic and bounded over \( B_\delta(z_0) \setminus \{ z_0 \} \). Hence, \( z_0 \) is removable for \( g \). Consider the Laurent Series then, \( z_0 \) must not be essential singularity to \( f \) as well. The Picard's Theorem states something stronger - on any punctured neighbourhood of \( z_0 \), \( f \) takes every complex value with at most one exception and infinitely often.

Analytic Continuation

We start with a simple statement - Given a non-constant analytic \( f \) where \( f(z_0) = c \) for \( z_0 \in \Omega \), then there exists \( \varepsilon \gt 0 \) such that \( B_\varepsilon(z_0) \cap f^{-1}(c) = \{ z_0 \} \), which can be trivially proven by considering the Taylor Series of \( f \) near \( z_0 \). We have a trivial corollary - if \( \{ z_n \} \to z_0 \) where \( f(z_n) = 0 \) for all \( n \in \mathbb{N} \), then \( f \equiv 0 \) by isolation of point in preimage.

Here comes another corollary - Suppose \( f \) and \( g \) are analytic on \( \Omega \). If \( \{ z_n \} \to z_0 \) where \( f(z_n) = g(z_n) \) for all \( n \in \mathbb{N} \), then \( f \equiv g \) on \( \Omega \). What is the importance of this? For analytic \( f \) and \( F \) and \( \Omega' \) and \( \Omega \) respectively and \( \Omega' \subset \Omega \), if \( f \equiv F \) on \( \Omega' \), then such \( F \) is unique! This \( F \) is called the analytic continuation of \( f \). We may "merge" two analytic functions when their intersection is non-empty as well.

The reflection principle is an interesting application of the analytic continuation. Suppose \( \Omega \) is symmetric with respect to the real axis, or \( \Omega = \Omega^+ \cup I \cup \Omega^- \) where \( I = \Omega \cap \R \). For analytic \( f \), we have \( \conj{f(z)} = f(\conj{z}) \) for all \( z \in \Omega \) iff \( f \) is real-valued on \( I \). The forward direction is trivial - whereas for the backward direction, we might as well construct the function \( g \) which the value are actually reflected across \( I \), which then we can assert \( f \equiv g \) via the power of analytic continuation.

The Theory of Residue

Residue

Given a Laurent Series \( f(z) = \sum_{n} c_n (z - z_0)^n \), we can define the residue of \( f \) at \( z_0 \) as \[ \Res(f; z_0) = c_{-1} \] Clearly we also have \[ \oint_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 2 \pi i \Res(f; z_0) \] Over a punctured disc. More generally, if \( f \) is analytic on \( \overline{\Omega} \setminus \{ z_1, \dots, z_n \} \), then \[ \oint_\gamma f(z) \, dz = 2 \pi i \sum_{k = 1}^{n} \Res(f; z_k) \] Which is just a simple application of the Cauchy-Goursat Theorem. We may also consider the residue at \( \infty \). In specific, we have \[ \Res(f; \infty) = \Res(z^{-2} f(z^{-1}); 0) \]

Clearly if \( f \) is analytic at \( z_0 \), then \( \Res(f; z_0) = 0 \). If \( f \) has a pole of order not exceeding \( m \), then we also have \( \Res(f; z_0) = [(z - z_0)^{m - 1}] (z - z_0)^m f \), the coefficient of \( (z - z_0)^{m - 1} \) of \( (z - z_0)^m f \).

Turns out residues are superbly useful - as illustrated as below.

Improper Integral

An improper integral of a continuous function \( f: [0, \infty) \to \R \) is usually defined as \[ \int^\infty_0 f(x) \, dx = \lim_{R \to \infty} \int^R_0 f(x) \, dx \] And \[ \int^\infty_{-\infty} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{R_1 \to \infty} \int^0_{-R_1} f(x) \, dx + \lim_{R_2 \to \infty} \int^{R_2}_0 f(x) \, dx \] Here, we care about the Cauchy Principal Value as \[ \text{P.V.} \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_{-R}^R f(x) \, dx \] We can actually use residue theorem to find the P.V. of an improper integral, by simply draw a "half circle" onto the complex plane.

Formally, we may define \( I_R \) the straight line from \( -R \) to \( R \) and \( C_R \) the arc parametised by \( \theta \mapsto R e^{i \theta} \) where \( \theta \in [0, \pi] \). Then, we can use residue theorem to compute the integration over \( \gamma_R = I_R \cup C_R \), then subtract the contribution of \( C_R \). If the function is not defined at \( 0 \), then we need to use two semi-circles instead.

Jordan's Lemma states that, suppose \( f \) is analytic on \( \{ z \in \C : \Im z \ge 0, |z| \ge R_0 \} \) for some \( R_0 \gt 0 \), while \( \max_{C_R} |f| \le M_R \) for all \( R \gt R_0 \) with \( M_R \to 0 \) as \( R \to \infty \), then for any \( a \gt 0 \), \[ \lim_{R \to \infty} \int_{C_R} f(z) e^{iaz} \, dz = 0 \] We can pull out the \( f(z) \) to \( M_R \), so we only need \[ \int_{C_R} e^{iaz} \, dz = Ri \int_0^{\pi} e^{-R a \sin \theta + i R a \cos \theta} \, d \theta \] To be bounded for whatever \( R \). Taking modulus, we need \[ R \int^\pi_0 e^{-Ra \sin \theta} \, d\theta \] To be bounded. The rest is pretty trivial. Jordan's lemma come into handy when we try to handle stuff related to convoluting with sine or cosine.

One of the other thing that will come to handy is as follows: For function \( F(\sin \theta, \cos \theta) \) to be integrated in \( [0, 2 \pi] \), we can represent both using \( z(\theta) = e^{i \theta} \), then invoke the residue theorem.

Argument Principle

We say \( f : \Omega \to \C \) is meromorphic if it is analytic on \( \Omega \) except for poles. If \( f \) does not have zero on curve \( \gamma \) parametised by \( z : [a, b] \to \C \), then we may define \[ \Delta_\gamma \arg f(z) = \theta(b) - \theta(a) \] Where \( f(z(t)) = \rho(t) e^{i \theta(t)} \) for \( t \in [a, b] \) and continuous \( \rho \) and \( \theta \).

Consider a meromorphic \( f \) on \( \Omega \) with simple closed curve \( \gamma \), where \( f \) has no pole nor zero on \( \gamma \). Then the argument principle states that the winding number \[ \frac{1}{2 \pi} \Delta_\gamma \arg f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} \, dz = Z - P \] Where \( Z \) and \( P \) are the number of zeros and poles of \( f \) in \( \gamma \) respectively, count with multiplicities. This is a consequence of the residue theorem as well; If \( f(z) = g(z) (z - z_0)^m \) where \( g(z) \) has neither pole or zero at \( z_0 \), then \( f'(z) = g'(z) (z - z_0)^m +g(z) \cdot m (z - z_0)^{m - 1} \), and so \[ \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} = \frac{g'(z)}{g(z)} + \frac{m}{z - z_0}\] Noting \( \frac{g'(z)}{g(z)} \) is analytic at \( z_0 \) gives the desired result.

There is an interesting corollary as the Rouche's Theorem - Given simple closed curve \( \gamma \) and analytic \( f \) and \( g \) in closure enclosed by \( \gamma \). If additionally \( |f(z)| \gt |g(z)| \) for all \( z \in \gamma \), then \( f(z) \) and \( g(z) \) has same number of zeroes interior of \( \gamma \). The key idea is \[ \Delta_\gamma \arg (f(z) + g(z)) = \Delta_\gamma \arg f(z) + \Delta_\gamma \arg \left( 1 + \frac{g(z)}{f(z)} \right) \] And the result follows.

Finally, we introduce the open mapping theorem - Given a non-constant analytic function \( f : \Omega \to \C \), \( f \) always maps open set to open sets, which \( f \) is called open. Suppose \( f(z_0) = w_0 \) for some \( z_0 \in \Omega \), define \( F(z) = f(z) - w_0 \) and \( G(z) = w_0 - w \) (just a constant). Then for sufficiently small \( \delta \), \( z_0 \) is the only root to \( F \) in \( B_\delta(z_0) \), while \( |F| \) is bounded below by some positive number on \( \partial B_\delta(z_0) \). Then for any sufficiently small \( |G| = |w_0 - w| \), a root is guaranteed by Rouche.
