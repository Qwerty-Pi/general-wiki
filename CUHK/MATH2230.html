Complex Numbers

Introduction

We put our focus on complex numbers \( \mathbb{C} \). What are them? In short, complex numbers is an extension of real numbers such that every real non-constant polynomial has root, "completing" the real numbers \( \R \). We introduce the purely imaginary number \( i \) where \( i^2 = -1 \) or \( i = \sqrt{-1} \), which we can write \( \C = \R[i] = \{ x + yi : x, y \in \R \} \).

For \( z = x + yi \), we denote \( \Re z = x \) the real part of \( z \), and \( \Im z = y \) the imaginary part of \( z \). Clearly, \( z \) is real iff \( \Im z = 0 \), whereas \( z \) is called purely imaginary iff \( \Re z = 0 \). A way to visualise complex numbers is to draw it on a 2D (Argand) plane, which the x-axis is the real axis and the y-axis is the imaginary axis. For \( z = x + yi \), we define the complex conjugate \( \overline{z} = x - yi \) - essentially flipping the complex number across the x-axis.

How about comparison? Given \( z_1, z_2 \in \C \), how could we compare them? We say \( z_1 = z_2 \) iff both \( \Re z_1 = \Re z_2 \) and \( \Im z_1 = \Im z_2 \) holds. However, comparison \( z_1 \lt z_2 \) is not well-defined. We can of course customarily define some sort of order (say distance to origin, tie-break by angle), but that is not going to be very useful in general.

For \( z_1 = x_1 + y_1 i \) and \( z_2 = x_2 + y_2 i \), we may define \[ z_1 \pm z_2 := (x_1 \pm x_2) + (y_1 \pm y_2) i \] And \[ z_1 z_2 := (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2) + (x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1) i \] How about division? We can write \[ (x + yi)^{-1} = \frac{x - yi}{(x + yi) (x - yi)} = \frac{x - yi}{x^2 + y^2}\]

Interestingly, conjugation is "exchangable" with these operators: \[ \conj{z_1 \pm z_2} = \conj{z_1} \pm \conj{z_2} \] \[ \conj{z_1 \times z_2} = \conj{z_1} \times \conj{z_2} \] \[ \conj{z^{-1}} = \conj{z}^{-1}\] One may trivially verify them by computations. Alternatively, for the last two equality, we may use polar coordinates or Euler's formula: From power series expansion, we have \[ e^{i\theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta \] In other words, \( \rho e^{i \theta} \) represents an anticlockwise of \( \theta \) of the "vector" \( \rho \). We can write \( z = x + yi = \rho e^{i \theta} \), which the conjugate \( \overline{z} \) is just \( \rho e^{-i \theta} \), and \( z^{-1} \) is just \( \rho^{-1} e^{-i \theta} \), whereas \( (\rho_1 e^{i \theta_1}) (\rho_2 e^{i \theta_2}) = (\rho_1 \rho_2) e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}\). The equalities trivially follow. As a side note, we also have the de Moivre's formula \[ (\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)^n = \cos n \theta + i \sin n \theta \] Which is an immediate consequence of the Euler's formula. Essentially - multiply by a complex number \( \rho e^{i \theta} \) is just scaling by \( \rho \) and rotate by angle \( \theta \).

We call \( \rho = |z| = \sqrt{(\Re z)^2 + (\Im z)^2} \) the modulus of \( z \), and \( \theta \) an argument of \( z \). Clearly there are many choices of \( \theta \) off by multiples of \( 2 \pi \), and so we define \( \operatorname{arg} z \) the set of all arguments, and \( \operatorname{Arg} z \) the principle argument within the range \( (-\pi, \pi] \).

As a final note, the triangle inequality \( |z_1| + |z_2| \ge |z_1 + z_2| \) and \( |z_1 - z_2| \ge ||z_1| - |z_2|| \) holds as usual. (What do you expect?)

Geometry in the Complex Plane

An interesting topic for us to explore is - essentially complex plane \( \C \) is the extension of the 2D euclidean plane \( \R^2 \). Can we represent geometric objects in \( \C \) as well?

First of all, we can represent a circle centered at \( z_0 \) with radius \( r_0 \) as \[ \{ z \in \C : |z - z_0| = r_0 \} \] And an ellipse with foci \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \) as \[ \{ z \in \C : |z - z_1| + |z - z_2| = d \} \] Another interesting represnetation of circle is as follows: For a circle passing through \( z_1, z_2 \) and \( z_3 \), we know the locus of the circle is \[ \{ z \in \C : \angle z_1 z_3 z_2 = \angle z_1 z z_2 \}\] What is \( \angle z_1 z z_2 \)? It is just \( \arg \frac{z_2 - z}{z_1 - z} \)! Hence, we have another (more complicated) circle representation \[ C = \left\{ z \in \C : \Im \left[\left(\frac{z_2 - z}{z_1 - z}\right) / \left(\frac{z_2 - z_3}{z_1 - z_3}\right)\right] = 0 \right\} \] Similarly, to represent a line passing through \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \), we simply have \[ L = \left\{ z \in \C : \Im \left(\frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\right) = 0 \right\} \] Clearly any line through \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \) cuts the plane into two half-planes: and the sign of \[ \Im \left( \frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1} \right) \] tells you which side of \( z \) is of with respect to \( \overrightarrow{z_1 z_2} \).

In a sense, we can treat complex numbers not only a vector, but scaling and rotation operator as well - which makes representation of geometric stuff sometimes much easier.

Using this point of view, \( z^n \) is equivalent to apply the same operator \( z \), repeat \( n \) times. Given \( z_0 \), can we find its \( n \)-th root such that \( z^n = z_0 \)? Clearly this is equivalent to \[ \rho^n e^{i n \theta} = \rho_0 e^{i \theta_0} \] Which means the scaling \( \rho \) should be \( \sqrt[n]{\rho_0} \), but the rotation \( \theta \) can be any of \( \frac{\theta_0}{n} + \frac{2 k \pi}{n} \) where \( k \in \Z \), since we can rotate any number of complete circles of \( 2 \pi \). Therefore, for any non-zero \( z_0 \in \C \), there are \( n \) distinct \( n \)-th root of \( z_0 \). Specially, we call the \( n \)-th root of \( 1 \) as the \( n \)-th roots of unity, which exactly is just \[ \left\{ \exp \frac{2k\pi i}{n} : 0 \le k \lt n \right\} \] We call \( \sqrt[n]{\rho_0} e^{\frac{i \theta_0}{n}} \) the principal \( n \)-th root of \( z \), since we are using the principal argument here.
