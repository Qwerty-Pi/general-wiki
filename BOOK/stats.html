Statistics

Prerequisite: Statistics and Data Analysis

Estimation

Concisely, an estimator \( T \) is a statistic that estimates the parameter \( \theta \) in the parametric space \( \Theta \). For instance, if we consider random sample \( \{ X_i \} \iid \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2) \), then \( \Theta = \R \times [0, \infty) \). Previously, we focus on finding estimators that maximises the likelihood \( L(\theta) \), or is unbiased such that \( \E[T] = \theta \). Here, we have some more questions to ask: (1) How can we determine which distribution generates the random sample? (2) Can we somehow generalise the central limit theorem for estimators? Before discussing these, we need the ability to do some analysis.

Delta Method

Let's say we have an estimator \( T \) with \( \E[T] = \mu_T \) and \( \Var(T) = \sigma_T^2 \). Given a function \( g: \R \to \R \), then what distribution \( g(T) \) has? Assume \( T \overset{P}{\to} \mu_T \), that is, \( T \) converges to \( \mu_T \) in probability as \( n \to \infty \), we can approximate \[ g(T) \approx g(\mu_T) + g'(\mu_T) (T - \mu_T) \] Which means \( \E[g(T)] \approx g(\mu_T) \) and \( \Var(g(T)) \approx [g'(\mu_T)]^2 \sigma_T^2 \). Specifically, if \( T = \overline{X} \), then we have \( \overline{X} \overset{P}{\to} \mu \) by the Weak Law of Large Numbers, and \( \overline{X} \approx \Norm(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}) \) by the Central Limit Theorem. Therefore, \[ g(\overline{X}) \approx \Norm\left(g(\mu), \frac{g'(\mu)^2 \sigma^2}{n}\right)\] Note that \( E[g(T)] \approx g(\mu_T) = g(\E[T]) \) means \( g \) and \( \E \) commutes approximately as \( n \to \infty \).

Order Statistics

The order statistics of a random sample \( (X_i) \) is the sorted permutation \[ X_{(1)} \le X_{(2)} \le \cdots \le X_{(n)} \] How can we compute the PDF of \( X_{(r)} \)? Heuristically, for \( \delta x \to 0 \), we have \( \P( x \le X_{(r)} \lt x + \delta x) \) being \[ \frac{n!}{(r - 1)! (n - r)!} F(x)^{r - 1} f(x) \delta x [1 - F(x)]^{n - r} \] And so we have \[ f_{(r)}(x) = \frac{n!}{(r - 1)! (n - r)!} f(x) F(x)^{r - 1} [1 - F(x)]^{n - r} \]

The simplest case to consider is \( (X_i) \iid U(0, 1) \). With some computations, we can show that \[ \E[X_{(r)}] = \frac{r}{n + 1} \] \[ \Var(X_{(r)}) = \frac{1}{n + 2} \cdot \frac{r}{n + 1} \left(1 - \frac{r}{n + 1}\right) \]

Quantile-Quantile Plots

In fact, order statistics have non-trivial use. Given a continuous random variable \( X \) and its strictly-increasing CDF \( F_X \), we have \( F_X(X) \sim U(0, 1) \), and so \( X \sim F_X^{-1}(U(0, 1)) \). Hence, by delta method, we have \( \E[X_{(r)}] = \E[F_X^{-1}(U(0, 1)_{(r)})] \approx F_X^{-1}(\E[U(0, 1)_{(r)}]) = F_X^{-1}(\frac{r}{n + 1}) \), whereas the variance of \( X_{(r)} \) is \( O(n^{-1}) \). That means for \( 1 \le r \le n \), we have \( F_X(x_{(r)}) \approx \frac{r}{n + 1} \).

We can then plot the Quantile-Quantile Plot (Q-Q Plot) to check for linear relationships. For example, for the Pareto Distribution, we have \[ 1 - \left( \frac{\alpha}{x_{(r)}} \right)^\theta \approx \frac{r}{n + 1} \] Therefore, \( \theta (\log \alpha - \log x_{(r)}) \approx 1 - \frac{r}{n + 1} \) and \[ \log x_{(r)} \approx \log \alpha - \frac{1}{\theta} \log \left(1 - \frac{r}{n + 1}\right)\] By plotting \( \log x_{(r)} \) against \( -\log \left( 1 - \frac{r}{n + 1} \right) \), if \( (X_i) \) is really Pareto, we expect a straight line with slope \( \theta^{-1} \) and y-intercept \( \log \alpha \).

Information

Now we really do calculus on (open) \( \Theta \subset \R^p \). From now on, we let \( \hat \theta \) be the MLE of \( \theta \in \Theta \), and \( \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \log f(x; \theta) \) be the log-likelihood function. We also assume \( f \) satisfies some regularity conditions. Our question is simple - can we find out the distribution of \( \hat \theta \) via some variant of CLT?

First, let \( U = \nabla_\theta \ell: \Theta \to \R^p \) be the score function, and \( J = -H_\theta \ell: \Theta \to \R^{p \times p} \) be the observed information. For the MLE \( \hat \theta \), we have \( U(\hat \theta) = 0 \) under regularity conditions. By Taylor, we have \[ \ell(\theta) \approx \ell(\hat \theta) + U(\hat \theta) (\theta - \hat \theta) - \frac{1}{2} (\theta - \hat \theta)^T J(\hat \theta) (\theta - \hat \theta) \] So \( J(\hat \theta) \) is a measurement of how quick \( \ell \) drops when we go away from \( \hat \theta \). Since we can only compute \( U \) and \( J \) given the data \( (x_i) \), we define the expected (Fisher) information \( I : \Theta \to \R^{p \times p} \) as \[ I(\theta) = \E[J \sep \theta] = \E[U U^T \sep \theta]\]

The last equality is not apparent. Note that \( U = \nabla_{\theta} \ell = \nabla_{\theta} f \cdot f^{-1} \), we have \( U f = \nabla_{\theta} f \) and so \( \E[U] = \int \nabla_{\theta} f \, dx = \nabla_{\theta} \int f \, dx = 0 \). Now, we have \( J_{\theta} (U f) = - J f + U U^T f \), and so \( I = \E[J] = \E[U U^T] \).

With these calculus magics, we back to the original problem. Given that the MLE \( \hat \theta \) satisfies \( U(\hat \theta) = 0 \), can we CLT it? First note for random samples, \( \ell \) is additive, and so \( U, J \) and \( I \) are all additive. First, since \( I = \E[J] \), we have \( J \overset{P}{\to} I \) as \( n \to \infty \). Second, we also have \( I = \E[U U^T] = \Cov[U] \). Hence by multivariate CLT, \[ U \overset{D}{\to} \Norm(0, I) \] Now, we have \( U(\hat \theta) = 0 \approx U(\theta) - J(\theta) (\hat \theta - \theta) \) by Taylor, and so \[ \hat \theta - \theta \overset{D}{\approx} J^{-1}(\theta) U(\theta)\] By Slutsky's theorem (convergence behaviour is "normal"), we have \[ \hat \theta - \theta \overset{D}{\to} \Norm(0, I(\theta)^{-1}) \] We cannot directly compute \( I(\theta) \).

Confidence Intervals

Assume \( \dim \Theta = 1 \) (since I don't know how it works otherwise). With the asymptotic distribution of MLE \[ \hat \theta - \theta \overset{D}{\to} \Norm(0, I(\theta)^{-1}) \] We naturally have the \( (1 - \alpha) \) CI for \( \theta \) as \[ (\hat \theta \pm z_{\alpha / 2} I(\theta)^{-1/2}) \] Which we can approximate \( I(\theta) \) by either \( I(\hat \theta) \) or \( J(\hat \theta) \).

t-distributions

For \( Z_1, \dots, Z_r \iid \Norm(0, 1) \), we say \[ \chi_r^2 = Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_r^2 \] is the chi-squared distribution with \( r \) degrees of freedom. Note that \( \chi_r^2 \) has the MGF \[ M_{\chi_r^2}(s) = (1 - 2s)^{-r/2} \] And so \( \chi_2 \sim \Exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \), and \( \E[\chi_r^2] = r \) while \( \Var(\chi_r^2) = 2r \) by independence of \( Z_i \). A natural application of \( \chi_r^2 \) is when we try to find CI for \( \sigma^2 \).

We define the t-distribution with \( r \) degrees of freedom as \[ t_r \sim \frac{\Norm(0, 1)}{\sqrt{\chi_r^2 / r}} \] We may treat \( t_r \) as standard normal with fatter tails; As \( r \to \infty \), we have \( \E[\chi_r^2 / r] = 1 \) and \( \Var(\chi_r^2 / r) = 2 / r \to 0 \). Now you ask - what is the use for t-distributions?

Suppose we have \( (X_i) \iid \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2) \), and we normalise them via \( Z_i = \frac{X_i - \mu}{\sigma} \) so that \( (Z_i) \iid \Norm(0, 1) \). We have the sample mean \[ \overline{Z} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^{n} Z_i\] and the sample variance \[ S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \left(\sum_{i = 1}^{n} Z_i^2 - n \overline{Z}^2 \right) \] Consider any orthgonal \( A \) where \( A_{1j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \) and take \( Y = AZ \). Then, \( Y \) is standard MVN as well with \( Y_1 = \sqrt{n} \overline{Z} \) and \( \sum Y_i^2 = \sum Z_i^2 \) since \( A \) is orthogonal. Hence, \( \overline{X} \) and \( S^2 \) are independent and \[ S^2 = \frac{\chi_{n - 1}^2}{n - 1} \] That means when we try to approximate the variance via sample, we actually get the t-distribution instead of normal - where \[ \frac{\overline{X} - \mu}{S / \sqrt{n}} \sim \frac{\Norm(0, \frac{\sigma^2}{n})}{S / \sqrt{n}} \sim \frac{\Norm(0, 1)}{S / \sigma} \sim t_{n - 1} \]

Hypothesis Testing

Imagine you have invented a drug that claims to help people to get more sleep. How shall you scientifically test this hypothesis? We usually denote \( H_0 \) as the null hypothesis, that is, the drug is useless; and denote \( H_1 \) as the alternative hypothesis, that is, the drug is useful.

As a normal example, say \( (X_i) \iid \Norm(\mu, \sigma^2) \) and the hypotheses are \( H_0 : \mu = \mu_0 \) and \( H_1 : \mu \gt \mu_0 \) where \( \sigma^2 \) is unknown. Then we can compute \( t_{obs} = \frac{\overline{X} - \mu_0}{S / \sqrt{n}} \), which the \( p \)-value is defined as \( \P(t_{n - 1} \ge t_{obs}) \). Obviously when \( \sigma^2 \) is known, we can use the normal distribution instead. When the \( p \)-value is very small, then we are confident in rejecting the null hypothesis; any \( p \) is less than \( 1 \sim 10 \% \) is very strong to weak evidence against \( H_0 \).

Obviously, we can have one-sided (\( H_1 : \mu \gt \mu_0 \)) and two-sided (\( H_1 : \mu \ne \mu_0 \)) alternative hypotheses. We can simply compute their p-values correspondingly, where absolute values are taken for two-sided alternative hypotheses.

General Setup

More generally, suppose \( (X_i) \) is a random sample with parametric space \( \Theta \). Then the null hypothesis is \( H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0 \) and the alternative hypothesis is \( H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1 \), where \( \Theta_0 \) and \( \Theta_1 \) are disjoint. Our target is to craft a test statistic \( t(X) \) such that the p-value \( p = \P(t(X) \ge t_{obs} \sep H_0) \) is very small. We say a hypothesis is simple when the set is a singleton, and composite otherwise.

What if we are forced to make a decision to reject \( H_0 \) or not reject \( H_0 \)? That is, we introduce a critical region \( C \subset \R^n \) such that we reject \( H_0 \) iff our observation \( x \in C \). To measure how "good" this critical section is, we define the size \( \alpha \) of the test as \[ \alpha = \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \P(X \in C \sep \theta) \] And the power function \( w : \Theta \to \R \) as \[ w(\theta) = \P(X \in C \sep \theta )\] Which ideally, \( w(\theta) \) should be near \( 1 \) for \( \theta \in \Theta_1 \) and near \( 0 \) for \( \theta \in \Theta_0 \).

Consider the simplest case where both \( H_0 : \theta = \theta_0 \) and \( H_1: \theta = \theta_1 \) are simple. Turns out for a fixed \( \alpha \), the critical region \( C \) that maximises \( w(\theta_1) \) is of the form \[ C = \left\{ x \in X \left| \frac{L(\theta_0; x)}{L(\theta_1; x)} \right. \le k \right\} \] Consider any other critical region \( A \) where \[ \P(X \in A \sep H_0) \le \P(X \in C \sep H_0) \] Then for all \( x \in \R^n \), \[ (1_C - 1_A) \left(L(\theta_1; x) - \frac{1}{k} L(\theta_0; x)\right) \ge 0 \] And so \[ \P(X \in A \sep H_1) \le \P(X \in C \sep H_1) \] This is the Neyman-Pearson Lemma. This lemma essentially gives us the most powerful critical region.

Now consider simple \( H_0 : \theta = \theta_0 \) and composite \( H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1 \). If the critical region \( C \) computed via NP lemma is the same for any \( \theta_1 \in \Theta_1 \), then we say \( C \) is uniformly most powerful (UMP) for testing \( H_0 \) against \( H_1 \).

Likelihood Ratio Test

Suppose we are testing the null hypothesis \( H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0 \) against the general alternative \( H_1: \theta \in \Theta_1 \). By defining the likelihood ratio \[ \lambda(x) = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} L(\theta; x)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} L(\theta; x)} \] Then the LRT uses the critical region of the form \( C = \{ x \sep \lambda(x) \le k \} \). For size \( \alpha \) test, we need \( k \) such that \[ \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \P(\lambda(X) \le k \sep \theta) = \alpha \] For the sake of simplicity, we define \[ \Lambda(X) = -2 \log \lambda(X) = 2 \left(\sup_{\theta \in \Theta} \ell(\theta) - \sup_{\theta \in \Theta_0} \ell(\theta) \right) \] The magical claim is that, as \( n \to \infty \), \[ \Lambda(X) \overset{D}{\to} \chi_p^2 \] where \( p = \dim \Theta - \dim \Theta_0 \). Recall that by Taylor we have \[ 2(\ell(\hat \theta) - \ell(\theta)) \approx (\hat \theta - \theta)^T J(\hat \theta) (\hat \theta - \theta) \approx \chi_{\dim \Theta}^2\] Hence, heuristically we can "cancel out" \( \dim \Theta_0 \) dimensions from \( \chi_{\dim \Theta}^2 \) and get \( \Lambda(X) \overset{D}{\to} \chi_p^2 \).

Hypothesis Testing on Categories

Suppose we have a categorial distribution \( Z \) where \( \P(Z = i) = \pi_i \) for \( 1 \le i \le k \) where \( \sum \pi_i = 1 \). How shall we test for null hypothesis \( H_0 : \theta \in \Theta \) where \( \pi = \pi(\theta) \) against the general alternative \( H_1 \) where only restriction is \( \sum \pi_i = 1 \)? Note that \[ \ell(\pi) = \sum_{i = 1}^{k} n_i \log \pi_i + C\] for some constant \( C \), so \[ \Lambda = 2 \sum_{i = 1}^{k} n_i \log \left( \frac{n_i}{n \pi_i(\hat \theta)} \right) \] Note \( \Lambda \overset{D}{\to} \chi_{k - 1 - \dim \Theta} \) and so we are done.

Alternatively, by writing \( O_i = n_i \) and \( E_i = n \pi_i(\hat \theta) \), we have \[ \Lambda = 2 \sum O_i \log\left( \frac{O_i}{E_i} \right) \approx \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i} \] which is the Pearson's chi-squared statistic.

How about we want to test whether there is "correlation" between two categories? The idea is the same: the null hypothesis is \( \pi_{ij} = \alpha_i \beta_j \) for some \( \alpha \) and \( \beta \), while the general alternative is just any \( \pi \) such that \( \sum \pi_{ij} = 1 \). We can similarly use Pearson's chi-squared statistics to get approximate value of \( \Lambda \sim \chi_{r} \) for some \( r \).

Bayesian Inference

Previously, we treat the parameters \( \theta \in \Theta \) as constants, and try to estimate its value with estimators. How about we treat the parameter \( \theta \) as a random variable as well, and "update" \( \theta \) upon new observations? Before talking about that, let's first introduce two distributions - the Gamma Distribution and the Beta Distribution.

The Gamma and Beta Distribution

For both of the distributions, our definition is simple: create distribution of a certain "shape", then normalise so that the integrated PDF evaluates to \( 1 \). The constant multiplier is less important, since we can always infer from the normality anyways.

For Gamma Distribution \( \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta) \), we want \( f(x; \alpha, \beta) \propto x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x} \) over \( x \in [0, \infty) \). Note that \[ \int_0^{\infty} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x} \, dx = \beta^{-\alpha} \int_0^{\infty} y^{\alpha - 1} e^{-y} \, dy \] By letting Gamma Function \( \Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^{\infty} y^{\alpha - 1} e^{-y} \, dy \), we have the normalised \[ f(x; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x} \] For Gamma Function \( \Gamma(\alpha) \), notice that \( \Gamma(1) = 1 \) and \( \Gamma(\alpha + 1) = \alpha \Gamma(\alpha) \), and so for \( n \in \N \) we have \( \Gamma(n + 1) = n! \). We also have the MGF of \( \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta) \) being \[ M(s) = \left( \frac{\beta}{\beta - s} \right)^\alpha \] Which \( \text{Gamma}(1, \beta) \sim \Exp(\beta) \).

For Beta Distribution \( \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \), we have \( f(x; \alpha, \beta) \propto x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \) over \( x \in [0, 1] \). To normalise this, we just lazily define the Beta Function as \( B(\alpha, \beta) = \int^1_0 x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \, dx \), and so \[ f(x; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{B(\alpha, \beta)} x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \]

As an interesting fact, we have \[ B(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha) \cdot \Gamma(\beta)} \] Which can be proven via some magic transformation. The MGF of Beta Distribution is not particularly nice, so it is not included here.

We reiterate that only the shape matters; once we deduce that \( f_X(x) \propto x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \) for \( x \in [0, 1] \), we immediately know that \( X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \), without ever computing the constants.

Bayes' Formula

As we said before, we want to consider the parameter \( \theta \) as a random variable over \( \Theta \). Formally, we denote \( \pi : \Theta \to \R_{\ge 0} \) as the PDF (or PMF) of \( \theta \). We know that \( f(x, \theta) = f(x \sep \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta) \), and so \[ \pi(\theta \sep x) = \frac{f(x \sep \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta)}{f(x)} \propto f(x \sep \theta) \cdot \pi(\theta) \] noting that \( f(x) \) is constant. We call \( \pi(\theta) \) the prior density and \( \pi(\theta \sep x) \) the posterior density.

If we take \( \pi \) as Beta or Gamma Distributions initially, they can somehow "absorb" many PDFs \( f \) due to their special forms where \( \pi(x) \propto x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1} \) and \( \pi(x) \propto x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\beta x} \) respectively. For example, Beta can absorb Bernoulli and Gamma can absorb Poisson and Exponentials.

Estimations

How can we do estimation with Bayes' Inference? First, for point estimations, we have \[ f(x_{n + 1} \sep \theta, x) = f(x_{n + 1} \sep \theta) = \int f(x_{n + 1} \sep \theta) \pi(\theta \sep x) \, d \theta \]
