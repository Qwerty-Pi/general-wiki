Electromagnetic Waves

In a region without electric charges or currents, the electric or magnetic field ($E, B : \R^3 \times \R \to \C$) satisfies the Maxwell equation where \( \Psi = E \) or \( B \) as \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \Psi = 0 \] Assuming \( F \) is separable, i.e., we can write \( \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) \times f(t) \), then \[ \frac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial_t^2 f}{f} = \frac{\nabla^2 \psi}{\psi} \] with each sides being constants. Naturally, we have \( f(t) = \exp i (\omega t) \) and \( \psi(r) = \exp i (k \cdot r) \) for some constants \( \omega \) and \( k \). Combine together, we have the wave equation \[ \Psi(r, t) = A e^{i (k \cdot r - \omega t)}\]

In words, \( \Psi \) is a sinusodial propagating wave with wave vector \( k \), the change in phase due to position; and angular frequency \( \omega \), the change in phase due to time. For EM waves, the dispersion relation is \( \omega = c \cdot |k| \), since EM waves travel at light speed \( c \). In practice, we assume that wave of any (valid) shape can be written as a superposition (sum) of multiple seperable plane waves, which kinda solves the general case.

We recall other concepts in waves. The frequency is \( f = \frac{\omega}{2 \pi} \), the number of complete phase revolutions per unit time. The wavelength is defined as \( \lambda = \frac{2 \pi}{|k|} \), the length of a complete phase. For EM waves, we have \( c = f \cdot \lambda \). Finally, the wavenumber (more in chemsitry) is defined as \( \tilde \nu = \frac{1}{\lambda} \), the reciprocal of wavelength.
