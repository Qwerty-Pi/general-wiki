MATH2020 Advanced Calculus II

Another thing in calculus that concerns us is integration. In higher dimensional Euclidean space, how can we define stuff like volumes and surface areas, or integral over a volume / surface rigourously?

Riemann Integral

Consider a bounded function $f: \Omega \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$. How shall we define the integral $\int_{\Omega} f \, dV$, intuitively "summing" $f$ over some subset $\Omega$? Well, it is complicated to consider arbitrary "shaped" $\Omega$, so let's first focus on the simple case - rectangles.

Rectangle and Partition

We define a rectangle $R$ over $\R^n$ as a set of the form $[a_1, b_1] \times [a_2, b_2] \times \cdots \times [a_n, b_n]$. The volume of a rectangle is intuitively defined as $\text{Vol}(R) = \prod_i (b_i - a_i)$. We can also define the diameter of a rectangle as $\text{diam}(R) = \max \Vert x - y \Vert$ over $R$. Let's say we subdivide each axis $[a_i, b_i]$ by $a_i = x_{i,0} \lt x_{i,1} \lt \cdots \lt x_{i,k_i} = b_i$, then we have a partition $\P$ of rectangle $R$. Note that partition themselves must be finite - although the subrectangles can be arbitrarily small.

For two partitions $\P_1$ and $\P_2$ of the same rectangle $R$, we say $\P_1$ is a refinement of $\P_2$ whenever $\P_1$ contains all the subdividing points of $\P_2$. Alternatively, for every subrectangle $Q_1 \in \P_1$, there exists $Q_2 \in \P_2$ such that $Q_1 \subset Q_2$. We can also consider the common refinement of any two partitions by considering the union of the subdividing points. Easy to see that one may treat the collection of all partitions as a poset.

Riemann Integrable over Rectangles

Let $f : R \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$ be a bounded function defined on rectangle $R$. The core idea of integration is to cut the domain into small rectangles, estimate each separately, then sum up the results. Let's say $\P$ is a partition of $R$. Then we can estimate the lower sum by $$\L(f, \P) = \sum_{Q \in \P} \inf_{x \in Q} f(x) \cdot \text{Vol}(Q) $$ And the upper sum by $$ \U(f, \P) = \sum_{Q \in \P} \sup_{x \in Q} f(x) \cdot \text{Vol}(Q) $$ Which essentially summing the extreme values over the subrectangles, which infimum and supremum exist since we assumed $f$ is bounded.

With knowledge on infimum and supremum we may deduce some inequalities. First, we have $\L(f, \P) \le \U(f, \P)$ for any partition $\P$ trivially. Next, if $\P'$ is a refinement of $\P$, then $\L(f, \P) \le \L(f, \P')$ and $\U(f, \P') \le \U(f, \P)$, since $\inf_{x \in Q} f(x) \le \inf_{x \in Q'} f(x)$ for $Q \supset Q'$, and analogous for the supremum case. Finally, for any two partitions $\P$ and $\P'$ of $R$, we have $\L(f, \P) \le \U(f, \P')$, since for the common refinement $\P''$ of $\P$ and $\P'$ we have $$ \L(f, \P) \le \L(f, \P'') \le \U(f, \P'') \le \U(f, \P') $$ And so $ \sup_\P \L(f, \P) \le \inf_\P \U(f, \P) $.

We can define the lower integral as $$ \underline{\int}_R f \, dV = \sup_{\P} \L(f, \P) $$ And the upper integral as $$ \overline{\int}_R f \, dV = \inf_{\P} \U(f, \P) $$ Which is the limits of the lower sum and upper sum respectively when we cut the rectangles arbitrarily small. We say $f$ is Riemann integrable when $\underline{\int}_R f \, dV = \overline{\int}_R f \, dV$, and in which the Riemann integral $\int_R f \, dV = \underline{\int}_R f \, dV = \overline{\int}_R f \, dV$. Simple analysis gives us the alternative Riemann condition for deciding whether a function $f$ is integrable, which is $$\forall \varepsilon \gt 0 \quad \exists \P \quad \U(f, \P) - \L(f, \P) \lt \varepsilon$$

Integrating Continuous Functions

What if we additionally require $f$ being continuous? Turns out that is sufficient for $\int_R f \, dV$ to be well-defined. Since $R$ is compact, we can cover $R$ with finite number of cubes $C$ where $\sup_{x \in C} f(x) - \inf_{x \in C} f(x) \lt \varepsilon$, which implies $f$ is integrable by riemann condition by considering the partition induced by the cubes. Alternatively, since continuous and compactness implies uniform continuity, and so as long as the diameter $\text{diam}(Q) \lt \delta$ for each subrectangle $Q$ in the partition $\P$, then the oscillation (difference between supremum and infimum) does not exceed $\varepsilon$.

With similar logic, if the set of discontinuity $D$ is not too "big", then $f$ is still riemann integrable. For example, if $D$ is just a singleton, then we can simply "encircle" the point with an infinitesimal rectangle, then the discontinuity would only affects a small volume. So the question now becomes - can we have some effective definitions for us to decide whether the set of discontinuity is not too "big"?

Content Zero and Measure Zero

Consider a set $A \subset \R^n$. We say $A$ is of content zero if for each $\varepsilon \gt 0$, there exists finite set of rectangles $\{ R_i \}$ that covers $A$ where $\sum \text{Vol}(R_i) \lt \varepsilon$, and of measure zero if countably infinite set of rectangles allowed. (Note that sum of uncountable positive reals is always unbounded.)

Clearly content zero implies measure zero, while measure zero compact set is content zero since we can pass to finite subcover. Also note that the union of a countable collection of measure zero sets stays measure zero by taking $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon \cdot 2^{-k}$ on the $k$-th set. Consider the boundary $\partial A$ (can be approached from both outside and inside $A$), then if $A$ is content zero, $\partial A$ is content zero as well by slightly enlarging the rectangles. However, such does not hold for measure zero sets, since say $A = \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]$ is measure zero but $\partial A = [0, 1]$ is not of measure zero.

In fact, a bounded function $f$ is Riemann integrable over rectangle $R$ iff its set of discontinuity is measure zero. Define the oscillation of $f$ at $x_0 \in R$ as $$\omega_f(x_0) = \lim_{\delta \rightarrow 0^+} \left( \sup_{x \in B_{\delta}(x_0)} f(x) - \inf_{x \in B_{\delta}(x_0)} f(x) \right) $$ And the set $D_\varepsilon := \{ x_0 \in R : \omega_f(x_0) \ge \varepsilon \}$. Clearly the set of discontinuity $D$ is $\cup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} D_{1/n}$, where $D_\varepsilon$ is closed for $\varepsilon \gt 0$. If $f$ is integrable, then $D_{1/n}$ must be measure zero by riemann condition, and so $D$ is measure zero as well. On the other hand, if $D$ is measure zero then $D_{1/n}$ is content zero, which we can simply consider the common refinement for the partition induced by this and continuity to show $f$ is integrable.

Riemann Integrable over Bounded Subset

Now we generalise the definition to $f: \Omega \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$ as a bounded function. To reuse the definition we have for rectangles, we define the extension $\overline{f}: R \rightarrow \R$ where $\overline{f}(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in \Omega$ and $\overline{f}(x) = 0$ for all $x \in R \setminus \Omega$, such that $\Omega \subset R$ any rectangle. Clearly the choice of rectangle does not matter. Now, we simply have $\int_{\Omega} f \, dV = \int_R \overline{f} \, dV$. Thus, $\int_\Omega f \, dV$ is well-defined whenever $D_f$ and $\partial \Omega$ are both measure zero. We define the volume of $\Omega$ as the integral $\text{Vol}(\Omega) = \int_{\Omega} 1 \, dV$.

Fubini's Theorem

How can we evaluate integral over rectangle $R$? An intuitive way is to maybe use the iterated integral of $\int_R f(x, y) \, dV$. That is, for $R = A \times B$, we can either compute $\int_A \int_B f(x, y) \, dy \, dx$ or $\int_B \int_A f(x, y) \, dx \, dy$. Normally we just blindly assume that they are all equivalent - but there indeed exists some problematic functions: For example $f: [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \rightarrow \R$ where $$ f(x, y) = \begin{cases} 1 \text{ if } y \le x \text{ and } x = \frac{1}{n} \text{ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$} \\ 0 \text{ otherwise }\end{cases} $$Then integrate along $y$ axis gives us $0$ for every $x \in [0, 1]$, but say $\int_B f(0, y) \, dy$ is not well-defined. What is the condition which we can compute the integral iteratively?

Here comes the Fubini's Theorem, which states that whenever $f$ is integrable on $R$, then all kinds of iterated integrals gives the correct result. Suppose we define $\overline{F}(x) = \overline{\int}_B f(x, y) \, dy$ and $\underline{F}(x) = \underline{\int}_B f(x, y) \, dy$ which are always well-defined. Then if $f$ is integrable, we have $$ \int_R f(x, y) \, dV = \int_A \overline{F}(x) \, dx = \int_A \underline{F}(x) \, dx $$ Why is that the case? By Riemann condition there exists a partition $\P$ such that $\U(f, \P) - \L(f, \P) \lt \varepsilon$, where $\P$ induces partition $\P_A$ and $\P_B$ in $A$ and $B$ respectively. Noting $$ \L(f, \P) \le \L(\underline{F}, \P_A) \le \U(\overline{F}, \P_A) \le \U(f, \P)$$ By thinking in infimum and supremum, and so we have $\overline{F}$ and $\underline{F}$ integrable over $A$ and give the same results. Trivial to see the estimation still holds if we swap $A$ and $B$.

Change of Variables Theorem

Another thing that we unconsciously do is changing variables. For example, to integrate $ \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^3 \theta \, d\theta $, any sensible person would think it is trivial and just substitute $t = \cos \theta$, $dt = -\sin \theta \, d\theta$ which $\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin^3 \theta \, d\theta = \int_1^0 (t^2 - 1) \, dt = \frac{2}{3}$. What exactly allows us to change variables in computing the definite integral?

Suppose $g: A \rightarrow B$ is a $\mathcal{C}^1$-diffeomorphism between open sets $A$ and $B$ in $\R^n$, which means $g$ is bijective and both $g$, $g^{-1}$ are $\mathcal{C}^1$. If $A$ and $B$ are bounded while $\partial A$ and $\partial B$ are measure zero (essentially making any continuous function integrable on $A$ and $B$), then the change of variables theorem states that for any continuous $f: B \rightarrow \R$ we can "pull" the integral from $B$ to $A$ by $$ \int_B f \, dV = \int_A (f \circ g) \cdot \left| \text{det}(Dg) \right| \, dV $$ Where $Dg$ is the Jacobian matrix of $g$. It may be confusing where stuff comes from. Intuitively speaking, we want to integrate $f$ on $B = g(A)$. Consider a tiny cube at $a \in A$ which get maps to $b = g(a) \in B$, then the vector $\delta e_i \mapsto \delta \nabla g_i$ when $\delta \rightarrow 0^+$. Thus, the original small cube is enlarged by $|\text{det}(Dg)|$ times. The value at the point is $f(b) = (f \circ g)(a)$.

Rigourously speaking, the change of variables theorem only applies on open sets since it is pretty hard to define differentiability when the set is not open in higher dimensions. For instance, for the domain $\Omega = \{ (x, y) \in \R^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le 4 \}$, we cannot apply the change of variables directly using polar coordinates by $g(r, \theta) = (r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)$ since $\Omega$ is not open; We can only cover $\Omega' = \{ (x, y) \in \R^2 : 0 \lt x^2 + y^2 \lt 4 \land (x \gt 0 \lor y \ne 0) \}$ by $\Omega_g = \{ (r, \theta) \in \R^2 : 0 \lt r \lt 2 \land -\pi \lt \theta \lt \pi \}$, where $g: \Omega_g \rightarrow \Omega'$ is a $\mathcal{C}^1$-diffeomorphism. The reason why integrating over $\Omega$ and $\Omega'$ gives the same result is just due to $\overline{\Omega \operatorname{\Delta} \Omega'}$ is measure zero.

How to prove the theorem? We will introduce the concept of partition of unity - piecing $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ functions defined locally together to obtain the unity on any open set $\Omega$.

Partition of Unity

Let $\Omega$ be an bounded open set, and $\{ \mathcal{U}_i \} \supset \Omega$ be an open cover. Then we can find a collection of $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ functions $\{ \varphi_\alpha \}_{\alpha \in \A}$ as the $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ partition of unity with compact support, such that (1) $0 \le \varphi_\alpha \le 1$ and the support of $\varphi_\alpha$, $\supp \varphi_\alpha = \overline{ \{ x \in \R^n: \varphi_\alpha(x) \ne 0 \} }$, is compact; (2) For every $x \in \Omega$, there exists a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ (open set containing $x$) such that $U$ only intersects with $\supp \varphi_\alpha$ for finitely many $\alpha$, where $\sum_{\alpha} \varphi_\alpha(x) = 1$; (3) For all $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}$ we have the support of $\supp \varphi_\alpha \subset \U_i$ for some $\U_i$ in the open cover.

Well, first of all, how can we use the partition of unity? Suppose $f: \Omega \rightarrow \R$ is integrable over $\Omega$. Then for any partition of unity $\{ \varphi_\alpha \}$ of $\Omega$, we actually have $$ \int_\Omega f \, dV = \sum_{\alpha \in \A} \int_\Omega \varphi_\alpha \cdot f \, dV $$ For any $\varepsilon \gt 0$, there exists a compact $K$ where $K \subset \Omega$ and $\text{Vol}(\Omega \setminus K) \lt \varepsilon$ by considering lower sum of $\text{Vol}(\Omega)$, and so $$\int_K f \, dV = \sum_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \int_K \varphi_\alpha \cdot f \, dV$$ Since $\supp \varphi_\alpha \cap K \ne \varnothing$ for only finitely many $\alpha \in \mathcal{A}$ from compactness of $K$, and so the result holds for $\Omega$ as well. In essense, we can combine "local" results into global results since the open cover $\{ \mathcal{U}_i \}$ is arbitrarily picked by us.

To find a partition of unity $\{ \varphi_\alpha \}$ of $\Omega$, define $\Omega_k = \{ x \in \Omega : d(x, \partial \Omega) \ge k^{-1} \}$ be the closed (and so compact) set where $\Omega = \bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{N}} (\Omega_{k + 1} \setminus \Int \Omega_k)$. Note that as long as we find some set $\{ \psi_\alpha \}$ that satisfy every condition except $\sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha(x) = 1$ but rather $\sum_\alpha \psi_\alpha(x) \gt 0$ on $x \in \Omega$, then we can scale $\{ \psi_\alpha \}$ to obtain $\{ \varphi_\alpha \}$.

Now, it suffices to solve this for $K = \Omega_{k + 1} \setminus \Int \Omega_k$. Since $K$ is compact, we can pass $\{ \mathcal{U}_i \}$ to a finite subcover, whereas for every $x \in K$ we can consider $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ function $\psi_x$ with side length of support $2\varepsilon$ centered at $x$ for some $\varepsilon \gt 0$ with $\supp \psi_x \subset (\Int \Omega_{k + 2} \setminus \Omega_{k - 1}) \cap \mathcal{U}_i$ for some $i$. By compactness of $K$, the open cover consisting of the interior of the cubes admits a finite subcover, which is exactly what we want.

Proof for Change of Variable Theorem

With the partition of unity for open bounded $\Omega$, we may finally prove the change of variable theorem. First, note that the theorem is transitive: say $g: \mathcal{V} \rightarrow \mathcal{W}$ and $h: \mathcal{U} \rightarrow \mathcal{V}$ where the theorem holds for $g$ and $h$, then $$ \begin{align*} & \int_{\mathcal{W}} f \, dV \\ =& \int_{\mathcal{V}} (f \circ g) \cdot |\text{det}(Dg)| \, dV \\ =& \int_{\mathcal{U}} (f \circ g \circ h) \cdot |\text{det}(Dg \circ h)| \cdot |\text{det}(Dh)| \, dV \\ =& \int_{\mathcal{U}} (f \circ g \circ h) \cdot |\text{det}(D(g \circ h))| \end{align*}$$ By chain rule, and so the theorem holds for $g \circ h$ as well.

Next, we attempt to show if change of variable theorem holds locally, then it holds globally as well. For $g: A \rightarrow B$, let's say for every $x \in A$, we can find a neighbourhood $\mathcal{U}$ such that $g|_\mathcal{U}$ is a diffeomorphism onto $\mathcal{V}$, such that the theorem holds for any $f$ with compact support in $\mathcal{V}$. We now have $\{ \mathcal{U}_x \}$ and $\{ \mathcal{V}_x \}$ as open covers for $A$ and $B$. By invoking the partition of unity on $B$, we obtain $\{ \varphi_\alpha \}$ of compact support, while $\{ \varphi_\alpha \circ g \}$ forms partition of unity of $A$. Thus, $$ \begin{align*} & \int_B f \, dV \\ =& \sum_\alpha \int_\mathcal{V} f \cdot \varphi_\alpha \, dV \\ =& \sum_{\alpha} \int_\mathcal{U} (f \circ g) \cdot (\varphi_\alpha \circ g) \cdot |\text{det}(Dg)|\, dV \\ =& \int_A (f \circ g) \cdot |\text{det}(Dg)| \, dV \end{align*}$$

Finally, we wish to show by induction that the theorem holds for diffeomorphisms $g: A \rightarrow B$ where $g(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = (g_1, g_2, \dots, x_n)$. For fixed $x_n$, we take $\tilde{g} (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{n - 1}) = (g_1, g_2, \dots, g_{n - 1})$, noting $|\text{det}(Dg)| = |\text{det}(D\tilde{g})| \ne 0$. Thus, $$ \begin{align*} & \int_B f \, dV \\ =& \int_{a_n}^{b_n} \int_{B_t} f \, dV \\ =& \int_{a_n}^{b_n} \int_{A_t} (f \circ g) \cdot |\text{det}(D\tilde{g})| \, dV \\ =& \int_A (f \circ g) \cdot |\text{det}(Dg)| \, dV \end{align*}$$

And so it suffices for us to write each $g: A \rightarrow B$ as composition of diffeomorphisms of this special form, locally. We claim that this can be done without proof here.

Integral Derivatives

With the definition of Riemann integral in $\R^n$ in mind, we turn our focus onto the notion of line integral and surface integral over $\R^n$. For example, how can one compute the length of a curve or the area of a surface, or the force done on an object along its path and the amount of wind pass through a surface?

Line Integrals

We define a regular parametised curve as a $\C^1$ function $\gamma: [a, b] \rightarrow \R^n$ where $\gamma'(t) \ne 0$ for $t \in [a, b]$. We say $\gamma'(t)$ the tangent vector, whereas $T(t) := \frac{\gamma'(t)}{\Vert \gamma'(t) \Vert}$ is the unit tangent vector.

For function $f: \R^n \rightarrow \R$ and parametised curve $\gamma: [a, b] \rightarrow \R^n$, we define the integral of $f$ on curve $\gamma$ as $$ \int_\gamma f \, ds = \int_a^b f(\gamma(t)) \cdot \Vert \gamma'(t) \Vert \, dt $$ By change of variable theorem, easy to see only the image of $\gamma$ matters (given that the curve is simple). We define the length of $\gamma$ as $\int_\gamma 1 \, ds$.

Similarly, for a vector field $F: \R^n \rightarrow \R^n$ and parametised simple curve $\gamma: [a, b] \rightarrow \R^n$, we can define the line integral of $F$ along $\gamma$ as $$ \int_\gamma F \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_a^b F(\gamma(t)) \cdot \gamma'(t) \, dt$$ Which dot product is taken between $F$ and the tangent vector $\gamma'(t)$. Note that $$\int_a^b (F \circ \gamma) \cdot \gamma' \, dt = \int_\gamma F \cdot T \, ds$$ So line integrals are independent of (simple) parametisations as well, except for the orientation of $\gamma$, which affects the sign. We denote the curve with the opposite oritentation by $-\gamma$.

Conservative Vector Field

With the notion of line integrals in mind, for any $\C^1$ function $f: \mathcal{U} \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R$ where $\mathcal{U}$ open, we define the gradient of $f$ as $$ \nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right) $$ Which measures the "change" of $f$ along each directions. Intuitively, let $\gamma: [a, b] \rightarrow \R^n$, then the line integral $$ \int_\gamma \nabla f \cdot d\vec{r} = f(\gamma(b)) - f(\gamma(a)) $$ Which is just the multivariable calculus version of the fundamental theorem of calculus. Indeed, it can be simply proven with chain rule and exactly fundamental theorem of calculus. If $F = \nabla f$, then we say $f$ is a potential function of $F$.

For the vector field $F: \mathcal{U} \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R^n$ continuous where $\mathcal{U}$ open, then $F$ is said to be conservative iff the following equivalent condition holds (1) $\oint_\gamma F \cdot d\vec{r} = 0$ for any closed curve $\gamma$, (2) $\int_C F \cdot d\vec{r}$ is path-independent, or (3) $F = \nabla f$ for some $\C^1$ function $f: \U \rightarrow \R$. It is trivial to prove the above are equivalent (well, compare to the previous proofs).

Green's Theorem

Now we be more specific, and consider line integrals in $\R^2$. Consider a $\C^1$ vector field $F: \U \subset \R^2 \rightarrow \R^2$ where $\U$ open, we can write $F = (P, Q)$ and $d\vec{r} = (dx, dy)$, and so $$ \int_\gamma F \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_\gamma P \, dx + Q \, dy $$ One may wonder - why we can "write" $d\vec{r} = (dx, dy)$? What does $(dx, dy)$ really means? Indeed, we can treat $dx$ and $dy$ as linear functionals in $\mathcal{L}(\R^2, \R)$, where $dx(x, y) = x$ and $dy(x, y) = y$, which $dx$ and $dy$ is used to capture the change of $\gamma(t)$, that is, $\gamma'(t)$.

For compact $\Omega \subset \U$ with piecewise $\C^1$ boundary $C = \partial \Omega$ where $\Omega$ is on the left of $C$, then we have the Green's Theorem $$ \int_C P \, dx + Q \, dy = \iint_\Omega \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA$$ Note that $\partial \Omega$ can be disjoint, where boundary outside $\Omega$ should be anticlockwise, where boundary inside $\Omega$ should be clockwise. The most interesting application perhaps using it to compute the area - simply take any $F = (P, Q)$ such that $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 1$, say $F = (0, x)$, $(-y, 0)$ or $(-\frac{y}{2}, \frac{x}{2})$.

Clearly if $F$ is conservative, then $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$, or else by continuity we can find a closed loop with non-zero integral by Green. Does the opposite direction holds? Well, only when the domain $\Omega$ is simply-connected, that is, every closed curve does not "contain" points outside.

Surface Integral

How can we define a surface? Similar to the case of curves, we define a regular parametised surface $g: \U \subset \R^2 \rightarrow \R^3$ where $\U$ is open and bounded, $g$ is $\C^1$ and injective, whereas $Dg$ is full-rank thoughout $\U$. We denote the surface $S := g(\U)$. Why we want the Jacobian $Dg$ to be full-rank? Intuitively, when we move in $\U$, the tangent space is $\left\langle \frac{\partial g}{\partial u}, \frac{\partial g}{\partial v} \right\rangle$, and so we want it to actually generates a surface, instead of degenerates to a line or stuck at a point.

With that said, we can define the unit normal of $S$ as $$ \vec{n} = \frac{\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial g}{\partial v}}{\left\Vert \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial g}{\partial v} \right\Vert} $$ The orientation of the surface $S$ is a defined unit normal $\vec{n}: S \rightarrow \R^n$ which is continuous on $S$. A surface with such $S$ is called orientable, and non-orientable otherwise. A famous non-orientable surface is the Mobius strip.

Now, for a surface $S \subset \R^3$ parametised by $g: \U \rightarrow \R^3$ and $f: S \rightarrow \R^3$, then the surface integral of $f$ over $S$ is defined as $$ \int_S f \, d\sigma = \iint_\U (f \circ g) \left\Vert \frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial g}{\partial v} \right\Vert \, dA $$ Which the surface area of $S$ is defined as $\int_S 1 \, d \sigma$. Note that by swapping the parameters of $g$, the orientation is switched. We similarly can define the flux of a vector field $F$ over $S$ as $$ \int_S F \cdot \vec{n} \, d \sigma = \iint_{\U} (F \circ g) \cdot \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial g}{\partial v}\right) dA$$

Curl and Divergence

There are two more special differential operators - curl and divergence. For a vector field $F: \U \subset \R^3 \rightarrow \R^3$, the curl is defined as $$ \operatorname{curl} F = \nabla \times F = \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ F_1 & F_2 & F_3 \end{vmatrix}$$ While the divergence of the vector field $F: \U \subset \R^n \rightarrow \R^n$ is as $$ \operatorname{div} F = \nabla \cdot F = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}{\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_i}}$$ Note that the notation $\nabla \times F$ and $\nabla \cdot F$ quite describes what does it compute.

We denote $\Delta f = \nabla \cdot (\nabla f)$ is the Laplacian of $f$. By simple computation we have $ \nabla \times (\nabla f) = 0$ for function $f$ and $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times F) = 0$ for vector field $F$ over $\R^3$. A question one may ask is - can we generalise curl over $\R^n$? Turns out yes - and will be mentioned later.

Stokes' and Divergence Theorem

The Green's Theorem relates the line integral of $F$ on $\partial \Omega$ and integral of $\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}$ on $\Omega$. Essentially, "differentiate" the domain, or the function itself, should give us the same result. Can we generalise the Green's theorem to 3D? That's called the Stokes' Theorem.

Given a parametised surface $S$ on $\R^3$ with measure zero boundary $\partial S$. Then for $\mathcal{C}^1$ vector field $F$ defined on open set containing $\overline{S}$, we have the Stokes' Theorem $$ \int_{\partial S} F \cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \vec{n} \, d\sigma $$ Which the orientation of $S$ and $\partial S$ are chosen so that $S$ is "on the left" of $\partial S$. Note that the Green's Theorem is merely a special case for Stokes' - simply take $F = (P, Q, 0)$ and $S$ lies on $z = 0$.

Similarly, given an open set $\Omega \in \R^3$ bounded by measure zero surface $\partial \Omega$ with unit normal $\vec{n}$ points outwards, and $\C^1$ vector field $F$ define on open set containing $\overline{\Omega}$, then $$ \iint_{\partial \Omega} F \cdot \vec{n} \, d\sigma = \iiint_{\Omega} \nabla \cdot F \, dV $$ Which is called the divergence theorem. Note that the form of Stokes' and divergence theorem are very similar - and indeed we can generalise and unify these theorems!

Integration on Submanifold

How can we generalise the concept of "integration on surfaces"? We would need to generalise the idea of volumes, cross products and surfaces.

Differentials

A differential can be treated as a linear functional mapping from $\R^n$ to $\R$. By considering $\R^n$ as a vector space $\R^n = \langle e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n \rangle$ spanned by the standard basis, we can consider its dual space of linear functionals $(\R^n)^* = \langle dx_1, dx_2, \dots, dx_n \rangle$, where for all $1 \le i, j \le n$, $dx_i(e_j) = 1$ if $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. For example, consider $ \int_\gamma f \, ds$ with $\gamma$ a curve, then $ds$ is a differential representing the length of $\gamma$ covered.

We can extend the definition of linear functionals to multi-linear functionals: As a simple example, let's say given two infinitesimal vectors $u$ and $v$, and we want to measure the signed area spanned by $u$ and $v$ in $\R^3$ after project to the $z = 0$ plane. How can we represent this? First, note that the signed volume of vectors $\{ v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \}$ can be computed as $\det(v_1 | v_2 | \cdots | v_n )$ - so similarly, we can define $dx \wedge dy \in \wedge^2(\R^n)^*$ as a bilinear functional $$ (dx \wedge dy) (u, v) = \begin{vmatrix} dx(u) & dy(u) \\ dx(v) & dy(v) \end{vmatrix}$$ Which can be considered as projecting $u, v$ onto the $x$-$y$ plane and compute the area via determinant.

To generalise this, we can define the $k$-linear alternating map $ dx_{i_1} \wedge dx_{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} : (\R^n)^k \rightarrow \R$ where $$ \begin{align*} & \; (dx_{i_1} \wedge dx_{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k})(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k) \\ = & \; \begin{vmatrix} dx_{i_1}(v_1) & dx_{i_2}(v_1) & \cdots & dx_{i_k}(v_1) \\ dx_{i_1}(v_2) & dx_{i_2}(v_2) & \cdots & dx_{i_k}(v_2) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ dx_{i_1}(v_k) & dx_{i_2}(v_k) & \cdots & dx_{i_k}(v_k) \end{vmatrix} \end{align*}$$ Which alternating refers to the fact that swapping two differentials lead to the opposite sign, since it is defined using determinant. We define the space of $k$-linear alternating map as $\wedge^k (\R^n)^* = \langle dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} : 1 \le x_1 \lt \cdots \lt x_n \le n \rangle$. Clearly, we have $\dim \wedge^k(\R^n)^* = \binom{n}{k}$.

Similarly, we can define the wedge product $\wedge: \wedge^k(\R^n)^* \times \wedge^l(\R^n)^* \rightarrow \wedge^{k + l}(\R^n)^*$ with $(dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}) \wedge (dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_k}) = dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \wedge dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_k}$ which $(\omega, \eta) \mapsto \omega \wedge \eta$. Due to alternating property of $\wedge$, we know that $\omega \wedge \eta = (-1)^{kl} (\eta \wedge \omega)$, while $\wedge$ is associative where $(\omega \wedge \eta) \wedge \phi = \omega \wedge (\eta \wedge \phi)$ by breaking them down to the bases.

Differential $k$-forms

Define a differential $k$-form defined on open set $\U \subset \R^n$ as $$ \omega = \sum_{I} f_I dx_I \in \mathcal{A}^k(\U) $$ where $f_I$ is smooth or $\C^\infty$ where $dx_I = dx_{i_1} \wedge dx_{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}$, $I = \{ i_1, i_2, \dots, i_k \}$.

We can define the exterior derivative $d: \A^k(\U) \rightarrow \A^{k + 1}(\U)$ on differential forms as well, where (1) $d$ is linear; (2) $d(\omega \wedge \eta) = d \omega \wedge \eta + (-1)^k \omega \wedge d\eta$ for $\omega \in \A^k(\U)$ and $\eta \in \A^l(\U)$; (3) $d^2 = d \circ d = 0$; (4) $df = \sum_{i = 1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} dx_i$ for $f$ being $\C^\infty$. In essence, we are just "differentiating" a differential form.

In fact, we can use exterior derivate on differential forms to mimic every differential operators - grad, curl and div - which we have discussed before! Consider open set $\U \in \R^3$. First, consider $\omega = f \in \A^0(\U)$, then $d\omega = (\nabla f) \cdot (dx, dy, dz)$, the gradient of $f$. Second, for $\omega = F \cdot (dx, dy, dz) \in \A^1(\U)$, then $d \omega = (\nabla \times F) \cdot (dy \wedge dz, dz \wedge dx, dx \wedge dy)$, the curl of $F$. Finally, for $\omega = F \cdot (dy \wedge dz, dz \wedge dx, dx \wedge dy)$, then $d \omega = (\nabla \cdot F) \cdot (dx \wedge dy \wedge dz)$, the divergence of $F$. So we have $$ \A^0(\U) \xrightarrow{\text{grad}} \A^1(\U) \xrightarrow{\text{curl}} \A^2(\U) \xrightarrow{\text{div}} \A^3(\U) $$

Pullback of Differential Forms

For diffeomorphism $g: \U \subset \R^m \rightarrow \R^n$, we can define the pullback of differential forms a $g^* : \A^k(\R^n) \rightarrow \A^k(\U)$ where $$ g^*\left(\sum_I f_I dx_I\right) = \sum_I (f_I \circ g) \, dg_I $$ Where the value is $f_I \circ g$, and the volume $dx_I$ is mapped to $dg_I$.

Turns out exterior derivative $d$ and pullback of diffeomorphism $g^*$ are commutative, that is, $g^*(d\omega) = d(g^* \omega)$ for all differential $k$-form $\omega$. By linearity, we only need to show for $\omega = f_I dx_I$, which is true since $g^* d (f_I \, dx_I) = g^* (df_I \wedge dx_I) = d(f_I \circ g) \wedge dg_I = d((f_I \circ g) \, dg_I) = d(g^* f_I)$ where $g^* (df_I) = d(g^* f_I)$ by chain rule.

Integrating Differential Forms

We define a dim-$k$ parametised submanifold of $\R^n$ as a $\C^1$ injection $g: \U \subset \R^k \rightarrow \R^n$ where $Dg$ is full-rank on $\U$. A subset $M \in \R^n$ is called a submanifold with boundary of $\R^n$ where each point in $M$ is a submanifold locally defined by $g: \U \rightarrow \R^n$ where $g(\U) = W \cap M$ and where $\U$ is open in either $\R^k$ or $\R^k_+ = \{ x_k \ge 0 \}$. For each $p \in M$, we can define the tangent space $T_p M = \left\langle \frac{\partial g}{\partial u_1}, \frac{\partial g}{\partial u_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial g}{\partial u_k} \right\rangle$.

For a dim-$k$ submanifold $M \subset \R^n$ parametised by $g: \U \rightarrow M$, the integral for continuous $f: M \rightarrow \R$ is $$ \int_M f \, d\sigma = \int_\U (f \circ g) \cdot \sqrt{\det(g_{ij})} \, dV $$ Where $g_{ij} = \frac{\partial g}{\partial u_i} \cdot \frac{\partial g}{\partial u_j}$. The formula $\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}$ is essentially the generalisation of "volume" when the number of vectors $k$ is less than the dimension $n$ of the space, which the formula matches when $k = n$ the determinant one. Specially, note that when $k = 2$ and $n = 3$, we have $\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})} = \sqrt{\Vert g_u \Vert^2 \cdot \Vert g_v \Vert^2 - (g_u \cdot g_v)^2} = \Vert g_u \times g_v \Vert$, so is a generalisation of the surface area element. An orientation on $M$ is a continuous positively oriented basis of $T_p M$, and $M$ is orientable if such exists, which is whenever the exists nowhere vanishing $k$-form on $M$.

For an oriented $k$-submanifold $M \subset \R^n$, its parametisation $g: \U \rightarrow \R^n$ and $k$-form $\omega \in \A^k(\R^n)$, we can define $$ \int_M \omega = \int_\U g^* \omega $$ Which by change of variable theorem the parametisation doesn't matter. With partition of unity, we may define integral for arbitrary (compact) submanifold even if we only have locally defined parametisation.

Generalised Stokes' Theorem

To generalise Stokes, let $M$ be dim-$k$ compact oriented submanifold with boundary $\partial M$ with boundary orientation, which $\{ v_1, \dots, v_{k - 1} \}$ in $T_p \partial M$ is positively oriented iff $\{ n, v_1, \dots, v_{k - 1} \}$ is positively oriented. Then for all $(k-1)$-forms $\omega$, we have $$ \int_{\partial M} \omega = \int_{M} d \omega $$ Note that this generalise all three theorems of Green, divergence and Stokes.

With partition of unity, we only care about local parametisation $g: \U \rightarrow \R^n$ where $\U$, open in $\R^k$ or $\R^k_+$, contains $\supp \omega$. From definition we have $$ \int_M d \omega = \int_\U g^*(d \omega) = \int_\U d(g^* \omega) $$ Where $g^* \omega$ is $(k - 1)$-form on $\U \subset \R^k$, so we can write $$ g^* \omega = \sum_{i = 1}^k g_i(x) \bigwedge_{j \ne i} dx_j $$ And so $$ d(g^* \omega) = \sum_{i = 1}^{k} (-1)^{i - 1} \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_i} \bigwedge^k_{j = 1} dx_j $$

When $\U$ is open in $\R^k$ or $\omega = 0$ on $\partial M$, then by picking rectangle $R$ containing $M$ we have by Fubini $$ \begin{align*} & \int_\U d(g^* \omega) \\ = & \sum_{i = 1}^k (-1)^{i - 1} \int_R \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_i} \bigwedge_{j = 1}^k dx_j \\ = & \sum_{i = 1}^{k} \int_{R_i} \int_{a_i}^{b_i} \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_i} dx_i \bigwedge_{j \ne i} dx_j = 0 \end{align*}$$ And for boundary $\U$ open in $\R^k_+$, we have again $$ \begin{align*} & \int_\U d(g^* \omega) \\ = & \sum_{i = 1}^{k} \int_{R_i} \int_{a_i}^{b_i} \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_i} dx_i \bigwedge_{j \ne i} dx_j \\ = & \int_{R_k} \int_0^{b_k} \frac{\partial g_k}{\partial x_k} dx_k \bigwedge_{j \ne k} dx_j \\ = & \int_{\U \cap \partial \R^k_+} g^* \omega \end{align*}$$ Hence by partition of unity we can piece everything together to get the generalised Stokes $$ \int_{\partial M} \omega = \int_M d \omega $$
