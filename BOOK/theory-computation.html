Introduction to the Theory of Computation

Can a computer think? What can computer solve? What kind of problems can a computer solve efficiently and practically? All these questions are philosophical. But "computer" is a vague wordings - is a vending machine a computer? Or an electronic calculator? Or some sort of teletype printer? Or even abacus? As you can see - we need some sort of clear definition of "computer" and "problem" to reason about them.

Following Aristotle, we use computational models, a model that supports a certain set of primitive operations, as an abstraction of computers.

  • Part One goes through weaker automata, such as the Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA), Non-deterministic Finite Automaton (NFA) and the Pushdown Automaton (PDA).
  • In Part Two and Three, we focus on the strongest known implementable model to human kind - the Turing Machine (TM).
    • Part Two focuses on Computability Theory, which asks the question - What class of problems can be solved by Turing Machine?
    • Part Three focuses on Complexity Theory, which raises a similar question - What class of problems can be solved efficiently, measured in time or space, by Turing Machine?

All these questions can be somewhat answered via precise and elegant set-theoric definitions and deductions.

Part One: Automata and Languages

Formal Languages and Decision Problems

A finite alphabet \( \Sigma \) as any set of symbols such as \( \{ \texttt{0}, \texttt{1} \} \) or \( \{ \texttt{a}, \texttt{b}, \texttt{c} \} \). A string over \( \Sigma \) is any finite sequence of symbols of \( \Sigma \) such as \( \texttt{000101} \) or \( \texttt{ababc} \), where the empty string is denoted as \( \varepsilon \). The set of all strings over \( \Sigma \) is denoted as \( \Sigma^* \). Finally, a language \( L \) over \( \Sigma \) is any collection (set) of strings over \( \Sigma \).

A large class of problems that we will deal with is the decision problems - That is, given a language \( L \) and any string \( w \), decide if \( w \in L \). For example, let \( L = \{ \texttt{a}^n \texttt{b}^n : n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \} \), then \( \texttt{aabb} \in L \) and \( \varepsilon \in L \), but \( \texttt{aab} \not\in L \). We want our machines to be able to decide that for us. When is it possible, and when not? Of course, that depends on the language given, and, our choice of computational model. The more complicated a language is, then more strength our model requires to solve the decision problem.

Naturally, we want classify the languages according to how "complicated" they are, and the computational models according to their ability in recognizing languages. In Part One, we will go through Regular Languages (RL) and Context-Free Languages (CFL) as the simplest classes of languages.
